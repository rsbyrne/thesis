In previous chapters, we have discussed the knowns and unknowns of planetary evolution ([](#chapter-01)), dived deep into the mathematics of convection ([](#chapter-02)), and presented the novel tools and techniques necessary to address these questions at scale ([](#chapter-03)). It is well past time to actually run some models.

While our overarching question we are asking is of course "What kind of thing is a planet?", in practice, the object of our study is a certain kind of computational numerical fluid dynamic model. Our commitment to the larger theme - a real science of planetology - is reflected in our decision not to shape our modelling around any particular real-world scenario, but to instead seek to understand the model itself: how it works, what drives it, where it tends, and why. The *insofar* logical operator connects this approach to the ultimate subject matter of planets and planetary dynamics: we wish to be able to say, *insofar* as planets behave like balls of hot sticky fluid, we should expect them to be limited in *these* ways, and capable in *those* ways.

The moment we step away from scenario-driven modelling, we are beholden to return with something valuable to justify the excursion. Qualitative characterisations will not cut it; speculation is not enough. The objective of our approach - what you might calls 'exploration-driven modelling' - is to *map the territory*. This is, to put it bluntly, very hard. Mapping the two-dimensional surface of the Earth has proved onerous enough. Mapping a five-dimensional parameter surface is exponentially more daunting. Add in the unquantifiable - but certainly extreme - dimensionality of state space and you have a task that would seem to be quixotic at best.

Nevertheless, it is our intention to not only map, but to effectively *exhaust* the particular category of models which are our subject. And we will start by characterising the first and deepest boundary through the parameter space of any convecting system: the boundary between convection and conduction.

The model we have chosen for our foray into the unknown - and it is far from the only one we could have chosen - is based on the classic Moresi and Solomatov viscoplastic rheology, extended through several additional dimensions. The model is planar and dimensionless, with a Dirichlet-type upper boundary condition (set to dimensionless temperature 'zero') set over an undifferentiated fluid driven by forces of gravity and thermal expansion and retarded by thermal- and strain rate-dependent viscosity. The endmembers of this model effectively 'cover' the isoviscous and Arrhenius-type rheologies (which this thesis generally refers to as the 'linear rheologies'): consequently those rheologies will be explored too, though still with the ultimate intent of developing our understanding of the higher rheology.

The state space of our system is simply the space of positive real numbers mapped over grids of arbitrary size: the equivalent of taking an m-by-n chess board and writing numbers greater than zero on every square. The numbers of course represent (dimensionless) temperatures - the only value within the model that is 'remembered' by time - ranging from a minimum value of zero (the 'temperature' of outer space), through a notional reference value of one (typically the invariant core temperature), through to infinity. Considered in those terms, the state space is vast: for a relatively coarse resolution of 64-by-64 cells, we have 4,096 temperature values, which we must track through every integrative time step and ultimately project onto a time continuum. In practice, of course, the state variables are highly correlated: a cell that is very hot is very likely to be situated next to another very hot cell. Thus it can be more productive to view this array of variables as as collectively making up a single vector-valued variable, which in our code is always dubbed `temperatureField`. The vector can be 'scalarised' (collapsed into a single value) in many ways as we shall see. This transformation - more technically a kind of 'embedding' - takes an otherwise intractable multi-thousand-dimension state space and returns a much more manageable handful of dimensions, albeit with a residual component that is not easily quantified.

The parameter space of our system is thankfully much more compact. We generally follow after the orginal parameterisation, albeit with the addition of several new dimensions not originally contemplated. The classic model had five principal parameters:

- The overall convective vigour, understood as the *Rayleigh* number $\mathrm{Ra}$, though more literally entering in as the coefficient of thermal expansion *alpha* ($\alpha$) - the dimensionless ratio by which a unit of increased temperature results in a fractional change in spatial extent under constant pressure.

- Two parameters characterising the Arrhenius-type (exponentially temperature-dependent) rheology: `etaRef` ($\eta_{\mathrm{ref}}$) and `etaDelta` ($\Delta \eta$).  These two values provide the kinematic viscosity due to temperature throughout the domain, from a minimum value of `etaRef` to a maximum value of `etaRef + etaDelta`. Viscosity is a kind of diffusivity and so takes units of area per time.

- Two parameters characterising the strain-rate-dependent rheology: `tauRef` ($\tau_{\mathrm{ref}}$) and `tauDelta` ($\Delta \tau$). The sum of these values provides a value of $\tau$ throughout the domain, increasing with depth as (notionally) greater pressure at the base makes materials more ductile.

- Two parameters providing the spatiotemporal scales of the temperature field: the characteristic temperature scale ${\Delta T}_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and the thermal diffusivity $\kappa$. The characteristic scale is arbitrary and need not be absolute. The thermal diffusivity, like the viscosity, is in units of area per time.

The two pairs of viscosity parameters combine to define the hybrid viscoplastic rheology $\nu$, which in turn interact with the two temperature parameters and the thermal expansivity $\alpha$ to shape the system behaviour as a whole. An effective parameterisation will seek to minimise or, ideally, eliminate planes of identical behaviour: you could say that a good parameter space should be 'maximally orthogonal', in the same manner that a coordinate system in terms of East-West and North-South is more useful than a system in terms of North-South and Northeast-Southwest. The triple parameters of $\nu$, $\alpha$, and temperature scale are not orthogonal in this way: put differently, there are triples of values that can be assigned to those parameters that are superficially different, but will produce the same behaviour, with the effect of changing one value 'cancelling out' the changes in another.

The authors of the classic study chose to achieve othogonality by selectively fixing certain variables at carefully chosen values. First, $\Delta T$ and $\kappa$ were both fixed to values of unit, as was $\eta_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (essentially stating that the non-variable component of viscosity is negligible). Crucially, the distance from the top to the bottom of the model was also fixed at unit. With both the model height and one of the two diffusivity terms - in this case $\kappa$ - non-dimensionalised, we now have an unambiguous spatiotemporal scale for the system. This should guarantee that no two model runs using different parameters will turn out to be equivalent either spatially (i.e. one is a 'zoomed-in' version of another) or temporally (i.e. one is a 'slowed-down' version of the other) - though many other kinds of symmetry remain in play, which we shall encounter often.

The remaining potential non-orthogonality in the model parameterisation arises from the relationship between $\alpha$ and $\nu$. We will shortly see how these quantities can be related through the *Rayleigh* number, but *a priori*, it is evident that there will be a plane of symmetry here because of the common dependency on area: the first in terms of area per temperature, and the second in terms of area per time. Trivially, varying either of these two quantities in isolation will succesfully drive the model into new territory: in other words, there are no two values of $\alpha$ that will produce model trajectories that are equivalent in space or time. However, it is conceivable that varying the two quantities *in tandem* might have the effect of cancelling one with the other: specifically, we could imagine that a certain amount of additional thermal expansivity - and the buoyancy force thus generated - could be dissipated by a certain amount of additional viscosity.

The difference between the two apparently equivalent scenarios must tell, somewhere. After all, a force 'resisted' is in reality a force being communicated to (and lost within) 'hidden dimensions' - which in the real world would take the form of, say, intramolecular bonds or latent heat, and in the model world take no form at all but simply disappear into the background assumptions. In this case, the 'lost information' can be found in the stress field, which will record much higher values in the high-$\alpha$, high-$\nu$ scenario than in the otherwise equivalent low-end scenario. In a system where there is no dependency between the evolution of the temperature field and the stress field, there may therefore be a plane of symmetry - and thus redundancy, from a modeller's point of view - in the $\alpha - $\nu$ parameter plane.
