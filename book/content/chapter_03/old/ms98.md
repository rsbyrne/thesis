## The *Starling* model

While our overarching question we are asking is of course "What kind of thing is a planet?", in practice, the object of our study is a certain kind of computational numerical fluid dynamic model. Our commitment to the larger theme - a real science of planetology - is reflected in our decision not to shape our modelling around any particular real-world scenario, but to instead seek to understand the model itself: how it works, what drives it, where it tends, and why. The *insofar* logical operator connects this approach to the ultimate subject matter of planets and planetary dynamics: we wish to be able to say, *insofar* as planets behave like balls of hot sticky fluid, we should expect them to be limited in *these* ways, and capable in *those* ways.

The moment we step away from scenario-driven modelling, we are beholden to return with something valuable to justify the excursion. Qualitative characterisations will not cut it; speculation is not enough. The objective of our approach - what you might calls 'exploration-driven modelling' - is to *map the territory*. This is, to put it bluntly, very hard. Mapping the two-dimensional surface of the Earth has proved onerous enough. Mapping a five-dimensional parameter surface is exponentially more daunting. Add in the unquantifiable - but certainly extreme - dimensionality of state space and you have a task that would seem to be quixotic at best.

Nevertheless, it is our intention to not only map, but to effectively *exhaust* our model. It is to that sole end that we developed *PlanetEngine*, *Everest*, and the whole larger apparatus hitherto discussed.

The model we have chosen for our foray into the unknown - and it is far from the only one we could have chosen - is based on the classic Moresi and Solomatov viscoplastic rheology [@Moresi1998-az], which we will dub *MS98* for short. The *MS98* model is planar and dimensionless, with a Dirichlet-type upper boundary condition (set to dimensionless temperature 'zero') set over an undifferentiated fluid driven by forces of gravity and thermal expansion and retarded by thermal- and strain rate-dependent viscosity. The endmembers of this model effectively 'cover' the isoviscous and Arrhenius-type rheologies (which this thesis generally refers to as the 'linear rheologies').

### State space

The state space of our system is simply the space of positive real numbers mapped over grids of arbitrary size: the equivalent of taking an m-by-n chess board and writing numbers greater than zero on every square. The numbers of course represent (dimensionless) temperatures - the only value within the model that is 'remembered' by time - ranging from a minimum value of zero (the 'temperature' of outer space), through a notional reference value of one (typically the invariant core temperature), through to infinity. Considered in those terms, the state space is vast: for a relatively coarse resolution of 64-by-64 cells, we have 4,096 temperature values, which we must track through every integrative time step and ultimately project onto a time continuum. In practice, of course, the state variables are highly correlated: a cell that is very hot is very likely to be situated next to another very hot cell. Thus it can be more productive to view this array of variables as as collectively making up a single vector-valued variable, which in our code is always dubbed `temperatureField`. The vector can be 'scalarised' (collapsed into a single value) in many ways as we shall see. This transformation - more technically a kind of 'embedding' - takes an otherwise intractable multi-thousand-dimension state space and returns a much more manageable handful of dimensions, albeit with a residual component that is not easily quantified.

### Parameters of temperature and rheology

The parameter space of our system is thankfully much more compact than our state space. We generally follow after the orginal parameterisation, albeit with the addition of several new dimensions not originally contemplated. The classic model had five principal parameters:

- The overall convective vigour, understood as the *Rayleigh* number $\mathrm{Ra}$, though more literally entering in as the coefficient of thermal expansion *alpha* ($\alpha$) - the dimensionless ratio by which a unit of increased temperature results in a fractional change in spatial extent under constant pressure.

- Two parameters characterising the Arrhenius-type (exponentially temperature-dependent) rheology: `etaRef` ($\eta_{\mathrm{ref}}$) and `etaDelta` ($\eta_\Delta$).  These two values provide the kinematic viscosity due to temperature throughout the domain, from a minimum value of `etaRef` to a maximum value of `etaRef + etaDelta`. Viscosity is a kind of diffusivity and so takes units of area per time.

- Two parameters characterising the strain-rate-dependent rheology: `tauRef` ($\tau_{\mathrm{ref}}$) and `tauDelta` ($\tau_\Delta$). The sum of these values provides a value of $\tau$ throughout the domain, increasing with depth as (notionally) greater pressure at the base makes materials more ductile.

- Two parameters providing the spatiotemporal scales of the temperature field: the characteristic temperature scale $T_\Delta$ and the thermal diffusivity $\kappa$. The characteristic scale is arbitrary and need not be absolute. The thermal diffusivity, like the viscosity, is in units of area per time.

The two pairs of viscosity parameters combine to define the hybrid viscoplastic rheology $\nu$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\eta &= \eta_{\mathrm{ref}} + {\eta_\Delta}^{1 - T} \\
\tau &= \tau_{\mathrm{ref}} + {\tau_\Delta} z \\
\nu &=
\begin{cases} 
\eta & \eta < \tau / D \\
\tau / D &
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
$$

Where $z$ is the depth and $D$ is the second strain-rate invariant.

This in turn interacts with the two temperature parameters and the thermal expansivity $\alpha$ to shape the system behaviour as a whole. An effective parameterisation will seek to minimise or, ideally, eliminate planes of identical behaviour: you could say that a good parameter space should be 'maximally orthogonal', in the same manner that a coordinate system in terms of East-West and North-South is more useful than a system in terms of North-South and Northeast-Southwest. The triple parameters of $\nu$, $\alpha$, and temperature scale are not orthogonal in this way: that is to say, there are triples of values that can be assigned to those parameters that are superficially different, but will produce the same behaviour, with the effect of changing one value 'cancelling out' the changes in another.

We can achieve othogonality by selectively fixing certain variables at carefully chosen values. First, $T_\Delta$ and $\kappa$ are both fixed to values of unit, as is $\eta_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (essentially stating that the non-variable component of viscosity is negligible). Crucially, the distance from the top to the bottom of the model is also fixed at unit. With both the model height and one of the two diffusivity terms - in this case $\kappa$ - non-dimensionalised, we now have an unambiguous spatiotemporal scale for the system. This should guarantee that no two model runs using different parameters will turn out to be equivalent either spatially (i.e. one is a 'zoomed-in' version of another) or temporally (i.e. one is a 'slowed-down' version of the other) - though many other kinds of symmetry remain in play, which we shall encounter often.

The remaining potential non-orthogonality in the model parameterisation arises from the relationship between $\alpha$ and $\nu$. We will shortly see how these quantities can be related through the *Rayleigh* number, but *a priori*, it is evident that there will be a plane of symmetry here because of the common dependency on area: the first in terms of area per temperature, and the second in terms of area per time. Trivially, varying either of these two quantities in isolation will succesfully drive the model into new territory: in other words, there are no two values of $\alpha$ that will produce model trajectories that are equivalent in space or time. However, it is conceivable that varying the two quantities *in tandem* might have the effect of cancelling one with the other: specifically, we could imagine that a certain amount of additional thermal expansivity - and the buoyancy force thus generated - could be dissipated by a certain amount of additional viscosity.

The difference between the two apparently equivalent scenarios must tell, somewhere. After all, a force 'resisted' is in reality a force being communicated to (and lost within) 'hidden dimensions' - which in the real world would take the form of, say, intramolecular bonds or latent heat, and in the model world take no form at all but simply disappear into the background assumptions. In this case, the 'lost information' can be found in the pressure field, which will record much higher values in the high-$\alpha$, high-$\nu$ scenario than in the otherwise equivalent low-end scenario. In a system where there is no dependency between the evolution of the temperature field and the pressure field, there may therefore be a plane of symmetry - and thus redundancy, from a modeller's point of view - in the $\alpha - \nu$ parameter plane. In the simple case of an isoviscous planar Cartesian basally-heated system, with all other parameters being equal, we would expect two systems to behave identically in terms of the velocity field and the temperature field over both time and space so long as the ratio $\alpha/\nu$ is the same, with the only discernible difference being the values in the pressure field, which will vary in the proportion of $\nu$. Indeed, empirically, this is what we find.

(codesnippet-nu-alpha-symmetry)=
```python
###############################################################################
from planetengine.systems import Isovisc as S
alpha = 1e5; eta = 10.  # `eta` can be taken for `nu` in this context
# Build and run systems:
s0 = S(alpha=alpha)[:0](); s1 = S(alpha=alpha*eta, eta=eta)[:0]()
# Will break if velocity fields are not very nearly equal:
assert not (
    s1.locals.velocityField.data / s0.locals.velocityField.data - 1.
    ).round(4).any()
# Will break if pressure field varies by more than factor `eta`:
assert not (
    s1.locals.pressureField.data / s0.locals.pressureField.data - eta
    ).round(4).any()
###############################################################################
```

The $\alpha/\nu$ symmetry disappears when there is any sort of dependency of the evolution function on the pressure field, which is the case in some popular models (e.g. @Stamenkovic2012-kh; @Noack2013-cx; @Fowler2016-cq). For *MS98*, though, there is no such dependency even in extreme cases, with the sophisticated branching viscosity function dependent on *strain* rather than *stress*. Consequently, there is an opportunity to orthogonalise the parameterisation even more than the original authors did by expressing the two viscosity functions as proportions of the thermal expansivity:

$$
\begin{align*}
\tau &= \tau^* \cdot \alpha, \quad \tau^* =& &\tau_\mathrm{ref}^* + \tau_\Delta^* z\\
\eta &= \eta^* \cdot \alpha, \quad \eta^* =& &\eta_\mathrm{ref}^* + {\eta_\Delta^*}^{1-T} \\
\end{align*}
$$

For consistency with the original study, our model code will be parameterised in the original manner rather than in this new *alpha*-dependent manner; however, we will make use of the alternative parameterisation throughout when necessary to filter out the $\alpha/\nu$ symmetry.

### Boundary conditions

In addition to the parameters defining the rheology and the basic temperature scheme, there may also be parameters defining the boundary conditions. Given that the evolution function of our model depends ultimately on the temperature field and the velocity field at any given time, and assuming a planar domain, we must specify eight boundaries - one for each wall of each variable. We consider two kinds of boundary conditions:

- Fixed in value (a *Dirichlet* condition)
- Fixed in gradient (a *Neumann* condition)

If we imagine that we are modelling the mantle of a whole planet (whether actually the whole mantle or just a small part taken to represent the whole), the side-wall boundaries should be set to be as free as possible: the temperature values of the side walls should be allowed to be dynamic, as should the radial velocity component. The angular velocity component (perpendicular to the side walls) must however be restricted if we have no way of tracking and parameterising the movement of material beyond the current domain. Establishing so-called 'free-slip' walls is akin to claiming that the whole-mantle circulation is radially symmetric: a fair compromise between realism and pragmatism.

We must still constrain the inner and outer walls. It is self-evident that the radial velocity vectors must be zeroed out on both these boundaries if we intend to say anything meaningful about mantle dynamics (barring some mechanism by which material can be, for example, injected into the mantle from the core). It would also seem obvious that the angular velocity vector on the upper wall should be free-slip, as the radial vectors are on the side walls, because it is hard to imagine any realistic scenario where the upper surface of the mantle would be significantly restricted from moving laterally by friction with a planetary atmosphere - even a very thick one - let alone the thin plasma of outer space. (In fact, some authors have experimented with an outer 'sticky air' layer with such a property, albeit more for numerical stability reasons than scientific reasons [@Quinquis2011-zs]).

The angular velocity on the lower wall, by contrast, is a matter to be considered carefully, as it depends on the picture we have of what the core-mantle boundary is really like. It is conceivable, for instance, that a very dense and very hot molten outer core, as possessed by Earth, could act as a lubricating surface over which basal mantle materials may slide with minimal stress [@Tronnes2018-ei]. It is also conceivable that the lower boundary could be both lumpy and sticky in a way that could substantially retard lateral motion [@Tanimoto2000-dw;Tackley2012-hq]. Both possibilities have been explored in the literature. We follow the original *MS98* authors in allowing lateral free-slip on the lower wall, not out of antipathy towards or even disinterest in the fascinating recent work on lower-mantle dynamcis, but simply in recognition of the fact that we can only tackle so many parameters to begin with.

We are yet to speak of the outer and inner thermal boundaries. The outer wall was assumed by *MS98* to be fixed at the lower reference temperature (zero in their, and our, parameterisation). This insinuates that, relative to model time, hot material brought near the surface will more or less instantaneously cool to the ambient temperature, which for Earth would conceptually be the atmospheric temperature (a variable quantity as we know too well) but for our purposes is simply a temperature so much lower than the mantle temperature scale as to be effectively null and invariant. This would seem to be a fair supposition for geological materials on geological timescales. Though we can imagine certain (very interesting) scenarios where this would not hold (e.g. Earth-like worlds with thick, giant atmospheres, like potentially Neptune), and though some authors have made (very interesting) arguments that the situation even on Earth could be more complicated [@Weller2015-ci], we are comfortable adopting this assumption for now in keeping with the original model.

The *inner* thermal boundary offers two reasonable choices, both intriguing. An inner *Dirichlet* (fixed-value) condition would seem justifiable for a whole-mantle model given the enormous heat capacity of the Earth's core. Alternatively, if we suppose that the core and the mantle evolve in tandem, we could conceive instead of a lower boundary across which the thermal flux, rather than the thermal value, is fixed (a *Neumann* condition) - potentially at a value of zero, implying an *insulating* lower boundary. The original *MS98* model considered only the first case, implying a vast hot core whose temperature is never meaningfully impacted by model events. Our extension of *MS98* instead provides a `flux` parameter, which can be set to either `None`, indicating a *Dirichlet* boundary whose value is set according to the chosen initial (thermal) condition, or a value of type `float` which sets the whole boundary to a uniform *Neumann* condition where positive values indicate heat flow *into* the domain and negative values indicate flow *out* of the domain.
