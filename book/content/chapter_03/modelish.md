# Artisinal modelling

A typical computational numerical model developed by a scientist is a single several thousand-line file (or an equivalently long 'notebook' of some kind) that carefully and explicitly steps through a hand-designed procedure. The model 'inputs' will typically either be data files on disk somewhere (which the model script may be pointed to using a hand-typed "input file address" string) or, even more crudely, a set of hand-typed constants - which will hopefully at least be bundled together at the top of the file, so it is obvious when they've been changed. Model outputs may be directed to an output file, again, with a hand-typed "output file address" string - or, for a really crude model, simply printed to standard output.

The modeller executes the script from the command line and waits until the output file appears. If the modeller wants to run the model multiple times with different inputs, they might change the input file address, or change one or more of the hand-typed constants. If they're working in a notebook environment, they might change the content of one of the higher-up cells (hopefully remembering to restart the kernel before running again). In either environment, the modeller must take care to change the destination of the outputs too, to avoid overwriting previous datas.

As a modeller grows in proficiency, they may become aware of other approaches that enhance their workflow a little. They may learn some command-line scripting, allowing them to schedule multiple scripts to run with a single command. They may also learn how to pipe command line arguments into the script itself, allowing them to pass in input values at runtime, or better, pass in filenames from a directory of pre-generated input files. They may learn some basic database management - either using manual Python wizardry involving append modes and lock files, or proper SQL-style database updating and querying, which straightens out the output side. At the upper end, the modeller may learn the challenging art of parallelisation - perhaps via MPI or Python's multiprocessing module, or just by brute force - and discover the joys of total resource usage. The modeller begins to recognise that, with increasing mastery of superior tools, the workflow is progressively evolving towards a particular endpoint: an 'ideal model workflow' that is maximally intuitive, maximally frictionless, and maximally resource-efficient.

This realisation, if it occurs, usually does so at the very juncture in the career of a modeller-in-training where they either:

a. Hit their PhD time limit and graduate to the oppressive environment of the postdoc, where time pressures are great and patience for training, let alone methodological innovation, is scant - or:
b. Run out of money and discover that their hard-won insights will be better remunerated in the private or government sectors.

In either case, the 'methodological ceiling' is struck; the modeller's model is either abandoned or passed to a new modeller-in-training, who will most likely spend six months stripping out all of the hard-earned but hard-to-understand enhancements made by their predecessor and begin the cycle all over again. In some of the longer-lived labs, this cycle may have already repeated itself a dozen times. Every few cycles, some peculiarly software-oriented graduate student, or perhaps some software engineering specialist brought in by aid of a grant, may capture a few strands of these perennially lost learnings and encapsulate them as a laboratory-specific tool, or perhaps even an open-source library; in a few very special cases, that new tooling may spread beyond the confines of its original laboratory and make an impact across an entire discipline. The pipeline is there, but it leaks like a sieve.

It is instructive to consider what all of this looks like from the outside. When an expert from a software engineering background looks at a typical body of scientific code, their reaction is usually one of incredulity at best. Why aren't you using SQL-lite for your data management? Why aren't you memoising your functions? Why is none of your code type-safe? Why is the core arithmetic not vectorised? Don't you know there's a popular open source library for this? Why are you using Python / Java / R / MatLab / Netlogo / Fortran instead of C / Rust / Haskell / Schema?

Such interactions rarely but occasionally lead to attempted collaborations; then come the rejoinders from the science-side. What are all these dependencies for? Why is my code asking me for a licence key? What does all the jargon in this documentation mean? Why is this 'quick start guide to Dask' taking me a week to read? Why does this software need a virtualenv to run, and what even is that anyway? What is a JSON and why can't I open it in Excel? Why can't I change this function logic in accordance with the new findings from Smith & Smith (2026) without breaking the whole program? What on Earth is a container?

# Programming across the divide: the examples of physics and biomed

Successful collaborations across the science-engineering divide do happen, of course: and the situations in which they happen are illustrative of why they more often don't. The absolute best examples come from the worlds of biomedicine and physics, for different reasons.

Biomedicine is, of course, by far the most well-resourced discipline in all of modern science. Deep pockets have bought biomedicine a dedicated corp of software engineers who specialise in research software specifically tuned to the needs of frontline researchers in the field. Additionally, the high stakes of both success and failure have disciplined those engineers to build carefully and thoughtfully, with very little 'cowboy code'. The discipline cuts both ways, as researchers are themselves trained and encouraged to build their work around the standard methodologies, generating a virtuous cycle where user experience informs design iteration, and both sides gradually come to share common intuitions and common dialects. Biomedical software runs the gamut from snippets of laboratory analysis code through to enterprise software packages, often with intuitive, graphical, code-free user interfaces. The standardisation and embededness of numerical methodologies in biomedicine acculturates users to more readily accept proprietary and closed software, which are sometimes viewed with suspicion in other disciplines, releasing market forces that channel the latest and greatest technologies into biomedical research, from hardware-powered visualisation to deep-reasoning AI.

Physics has deep pockets too, of course, but its success in computational numerical modelling is owed to a very different strategy: the total merging of the usually disparate skillsets of software engineering and scientific modelling. Modern physicists-in-training begin writing code during their undergraduate years, and no budding physicist makes it as far as graduate research without a portfolio of Python projects under their belt. At the graduate level, the crucial importance of good code to the larger enterprise is understood, and PhD programs with a purely methodological bent are encouraged and supported. At the higher levels, physicists who choose to specialise in numerical approaches are typically expected to be fluent in 'lower level' languages like C or Rust, far beyond the grasp of most researchers. With so many physicists developing a dual proficiency, even those who are less proficient in code are able to benefit from the tooling produced by their software-loving peers because those peers produce tools that are naturally sculpted to the contours of a physicist's work. The physics software ecosystem is blessed with large, mature, sophisticated, and overwhelmingly open-source codebases - not always as efficient or user-friendly as those in the realm of biomed, but still predominantly within the bounds of what computer-literate researchers can handle. Physicists are also leading contributors to numerical computing libraries like Numpy, benefitting all sciences, and have authored a deep and burgeoning literature in applied and even theoretical computer science, advancing the state of the art beyond the Academy altogether.

Biomedicine and physics offer two different, equally effective, models for raising the methodological sophistication of a field - but at the same time, present two different lines of analysis which help to explain why methodological standards are so poor in the 'hinterlands' of science.

The first limitation is of course money - or rather, the presence or lack of a generous licence to perform foundational research. Both biomedicine and physics sit within the top tier of 'valuable sciences' in the public eye, and consequently enjoy an extended and seemingly irrevocable licence to operate according to their own judgement. To adopt a corporate metaphor, biomed and physics are 'blue chip stocks': stakeholders expect these disciplines to invest heavily in capital improvements in pursuit of much greater returns over decadal timescales. Most other scientific disciplines are, by comparsion, 'penny stocks': stakeholders (typically grant bodies) expect measurable returns over timescales of a few years at most. In science, as in the rest of the economy, great innovations arise unpredictably in response to necessity or curiosity, but innovations yield no enduring benefit until they are secured by sustained, heavy, expensive, incremental labour; the 'big sciences' have the license to do this work, while the 'small sciences' do not.

The second limitation is cultural, or if you will, philosophical. Biomedicine and physics have in common a deep cultural commitment to 'data', both specifically and in the abstract. They are increasingly reductive disciplines which have delivered substantial breakthroughs via a 'core working loop' of data collection, model fitting, and inferencing. While conceptual and 'deep theoretical' work are of course celebrated and supported in both disciplines, researchers in these fields are increasingly comfortable with the notion that the best models may not be comprehensible to humans, and thus, the quickest path to novel insights is via the design and maintenance of intricate numerical computing pipelines. Time spent 'crunching the data' is not viewed as wasted, and specialists with various data-related appelates to their titles are respected and aspirational figures. In this vein, the distinction between the 'big sciences' and the 'small sciences' could almost be expressed, as in chess, in terms of 'Romantic' and 'Modern' science; or perhaps, as in manufacturing, in terms of 'Artisinal' versus 'Industrial' science. In either metaphor, the framing of the situation as an incomplete or uneven 'industrial revolution' is complete.

The solution often touted - to in some way reorganise the sciences so that lagging branches can emulate the successes of the more productive or esteemed branches - is a non-starter for several practical reasons, not to mention the aesthetic and philosophical ones. There is a reason why consolidation in industry is never quite complete: it is the 'last mile' problem in a thousand different guises; a thousand different last miles traversable only by a thousand different vehicles. The standard numerical computing toolkit - increasingly Python driven, GPU-accelerated, and cloud-hosted - has likely already made most of the impact it can make in most of the places where it can make it. In the last twenty years, more or less every domain of science has grown a 'data driven' wing to mine, at scale, the rich vein of inherited data in its field. The return on investment has certainly been extraordinary, especially with the additional fuel of rich new datas from previously unimagined sources, like LIDAR. Nevertheless, there is a widespread angst that the boom has run its course; that the low-hanging fruit has been plucked, and that innovation, once again, is slowing to a crawl, just when it is needed the most. That angst has lately emerged as one of the more powerful drivers of the towering hype engine of artificial intelligence.

The truth is that there are many questions within the remit of science that cannot be approached in a 'Modern' or 'Industrial' manner, and of those that can, the vast majority are either too broad or too narrow to support a 'Modern' or 'Industrial' infrastructure. These pockets of the sciences - individually small, but collectively vast - have no recourse but to succeed within a 'Romantic' or 'Artisinal' framework. Thus it is necessary to bring the mountain to Mohammad and reinvent the 'science stack' for the benefit of 'small science'.

# The counsel of the duck

To devise a model for modelling in general, it does no good to start with what we have and merely fiddle around the edges. It is an old proverb of innovation - often attributed to management theorist Oren Harari - that: "The electric light did not come from the continuous improvement of candles." Legend gives Henry Ford the rather pithier: "If I had asked people what they wanted, they would have said faster horses."

Clayton Christensen called this the Innovator's Dilemma in his famous book of the same name; like any dilemma, the best way forward is to grab it by the horns. In this case, that means recognising the good and valid reasons why so many workers in so many knowledge disciplines refuse to or struggle to avail themselves of state-of-the-art computational methods, while simultaneously identifying exactly where and how those methods would benefit those workers the most.

If we reflect on the imaginary case study we presented earlier, it is apparent that our modeller drifted off course as soon as they started spending more time thinking about how to run models efficiently than they did thinking about the actual design of the model (by that point, the subject matter the model was intended to illuminate had already become a distant memory). We might instead think about what kind of program the modeller might have written if they had started out already knowing what features they would eventually come to value - like being able to run many models covering a swathe of parameter space at a single stroke, or like storing their inputs and outputs together in a single tabular database instead of scattered across a hundred CSV files. If, as some programmers do, the modeller had articulated this feature set to a rubber duck sitting above their desk, what would the rubber duck advise?

In the years since we started developing this thesis, this 'rubber duck' thought experiment has become quite remarkably literal thanks to LLMs like GPT, Gemini, and Claude, and a skilled knowledge worker who knows exactly what they need built, but not how to build it, can prevail upon a robot to do the building for them. This has certainly addressed some of the user need that originally concerned us, but with two significant caveats: one, it requires that the user does in fact know what they want, and two, it requires both that the machine is competent to provision that need and that the user is competent to recognise that competency. Even if both conditions are satisfied, it is as yet far from clear whether the 'base case' productivity for a worker adopting such tools is in fact superior to 'traditional' approaches, even disregarding the larger concerns and discomfitures that arise from entrusting knowledge production to a machine.

Whether or not we choose to rely on AI, we are confronted with the same underlying issue: that picturing points A and B is not the same as picturing the path AB; that picturing the path AB is not the same as picturing the means to travel that path; that picturing the means to get from A to B is not the same as obtaining such means; and obtaining such means is no guarantee of actually employing those means successfully. A knowledge worker who needs to reach point B thus loses months or years building a wobbly car to ride a bumpy road; and the bespokeness of the journey guarantees that no-one will be present or able to help them when they inevitably break down.

The solution to the dilemma can only be to work it from both ends. The scientist must be equipped with adequate methodological nous, and the methodology, for its part, needs to be appropriate for the full range of situations the scientist might encounter. What's more, we need to accomplish this for virtually all the sciences at once, because otherwise we shall have no hope of developing sufficient capital, sufficient synergy, and sufficient communality to build, maintain, and develop all of the appropriate tools. We must build the sort of closed methodological ecosystem enjoyed by the physicists and the biomedics, without the advantage of a closed field of enquiry; a synthesis that somehow heightens, rather than flattens, the desirable idiosyncracies of its constituents. This is a task which would seem to be, on the face of it, paradoxical: a dilemma within a dilemma; a real Gordian knot.

# Severing the knot

If we look first at the problem from a software engineer's vantage, there seems to be no problem at all. There are standard solution and ready-made softwares for every kind of workflow, from broad, highly parallelisable data analytics procedures to intense, CPU-locked heavy integration problems. Going down that road, however, would require a top-down 'biomed-style' approach that most sciences cannot support. On the other hand, if we solely work the problem from the point of view of the individual scientist, we are going to end up with a failed version of what we have now, unless we also cultivate a 'physics-style' grassroots integration with computer technology.

What is required here is a sort of 'guided codesign' process. That is in a sense what the author has undertaken over the course of a PhD programme, acquiring the full skillset of a professional software engineer while remaining grounded within a natural historian's perspective. In this section, we will show how the two competencies can be harmonised to produce a methodology and software architecture that is accessible and useful to all scientists without feeling alien or foreign.

# Introducing Modelish

If we wish to forge on in this general direction, we need to break the 'model-model' down into its atomic parts and attempt to systematise the ways those parts cohere back into a useful whole - the analysis, in short, that must precede the hoped-for synthesis. Further complicating matters is the requirement for this analysis to be original - in its reasoning, in its metaphors, and in its general 'attachment points' to common sense - rather than specifically borrowed from a pre-existing stock, like computer science or classical epistemology - since any framework that would satisfy all stakeholders would be situated in the conceptual barycentre of the constituent fields. We need an entry point into the intuitions of the users we intend to serve.

Let us set our 'entry point' at the place where our idealised modeller started out: inheriting the laboratory script from their predecessor on day one of their PhD programme. Let us call this individual 'Eve'.

We can assume that Eve knows, in broad terms, what the script does: put another way, Eve knows what the inputs represent, and expects to be able to interpret the results. As for the script's contents, to Eve, they might as well be ancient runes. Eve sketches a diagram of the experiment as she understands it:

[Diagram: a solid blue box with 'lab script' written inside; an arrow points into the box from the left, labelled 'inputs', and an arrow points out from the box towards the right, labelled 'outputs']

To Eve, the script is what programmers call a 'black box'. Not knowing anything about the 'box' except its shape - in this case, the file extension, '.py' - Eve does some quick Googling, discovers the command line and its `python` command, and attempts to execute the script: `python labscript.py`.

Eve is rewarded only with the error message: `Command not found: python`.

Eve has learned something important about computational methods: the context also matters. Eve chooses to call these the 'coputs', since they are like 'inputs', but also different in an important way: they are non-negotiable, often implicit rather than explicit, and have a 'non-scientific' character.

[Diagram: the same as before, but now with an arrow entering at the top, labelled 'coputs'.]

Eve installs Python, enters the appropriate environment, and tries again. The script runs; the outputs appear; success.

While the script was running, Eve noticed a whole lot of text appeared on the screen, only to disappear at the end. Later, she realises that these correlate with the `print` calls inside the script, and finds that they can be piped through the command line into a log file. Eve finds that information contained in these logs is not strictly necessary for interpreting the outputs, except in cases where the algorithm has malfunctioned or 'misfunctioned' (veered away from the behaviour suggested by theory). Eve also finds that the logs are useful for 'profiling' the code, figuring out how it does what it does and monitoring what it consumes along the way. Eve dubs these 'byputs' and jots them down on her diagram:

[Diagram: the same as before, but now with an arrow point away from the bottom of the box, labelled 'byputs'.]

It should be emphasised that, thus far, we have described Eve's journey without any reference whatsoever to her particular scientific field or discipline. We have also avoided any reference to methodological frameworks that would be outside of Eve's experience: the mental framework Eve is developing is native to her experience, and her experience is close to universal.

The next step in Eve's journey requires her to boldly go into the script itself, which is of course written in a dialect with which she has no experience whatsoever. Using only the intuition from school-level algebra that `=` connotes assignment and `f(2)` has the sense of 'substitute the value `2` into the function `f`, Eve discovers that there is a rich internal structure to the program. Expressions that look mathematical in nature are assigned names that are not included among either the inputs or the outputs: in other words, there are 'intermediate values', often named for the functions that produced them.

Eve decides to jot these intermediate values down in her diagram as green triangles inside the box that represents the script, reasoning that - however they work - they are solely dependent on the things available inside that box (the inputs and the coputs) and only communicate with the outside through the boundaries of the box (the byputs and outputs).

Carefully tracing the values by name alone as they flow through the script, Eve extends the lines of her inputs from the boundary to the interior of the box (adding little yellow diamonds at the crossing points so they look less awkward) and connects them up with the bases of the triangles, then extends lines out from the opposite corner of each triangle and into the bases of subsequent triangles. Finally, she connects the lines all the way through to the outputs. She is diligent with her colour pens, ensuring that each line shares the colour of the thing connected to its base, and she also finds that adhering to a consistent left-to-right convention obviates the need for arrowheads, making things even cleaner. Although the actual behaviour of the functions remains opaque to her, by simply following the names from place to place, Eve finds she is able to get a pretty decent idea of how all those inputs at the beginning end up influencing the outputs at the end, just by looking at her diagram.

[Diagram: as described in the text; a solid blue box with solid yellow diamonds in the walls and solid green triangles inside, bases to the left and thus 'pointing' to the right, each bearing an arbitrary, short, unique name; coloured lines flow through the box from left to right, connecting the various elements.]

What Eve has drawn here is what we call a 'Modelish' diagram. The blue box is called a 'block'. The green triangles are called 'gates'. The connecting lines are called 'cords'. The yellow diamonds serve as entry and exit points, dubbed 'pins' on the left (ingoing) side and 'jacks' on the right (outgoing) side. The cords carry 'charges' (bundles of information) from element to element, visualised as glowing pulses - something like a simplified circuit diagram. Though the nomenclatural metaphor is vaguely mechanically-inspired, the operative intuition is intended to be tactile and accessible - the sort of thing a person like Eve might come up with without the benefit of special training.

Eve reflects on her diagram and is inspired to note down a few 'rules' to make it easier to create and interpret diagrams like this in the future:

- Cords are directional connections between the right or bottom side of one element and the left or top side of an element. Depending on the element, cord may or may not be allowed to connect an element to itself, and a cord may never be inferred to 'flow backwards'.
- Gates always expect at least one ingoing cord (otherwise they're really just describing a constant) and at least one outgoing cord (otherwise they're not actually doing anything of consequence for the system).
- A gate's behaviour is entirely determined by its name in the context of its immediate host block.
- A gate fires an identical charge down each of its outgoing cords as soon as it has received a charge from all of its ingoing cords; until that time, it passively collects charges without acting on them.
- Pins (inbound ports) expect at most one ingoing cord (otherwise their meaning is ambiguous) and at least one outgoing cord (otherwise they are redundant); jacks (outbound ports) must have exactly one ingoing cord (otherwise their meaning is ambiguous) but may have any number of outgoing cords (because any number of forward elements may depend on it - that is not its business).
- Sets of ports (like pins and jacks) behave in tandem with each other: i.e. no pin fires until all pins are charged, and no jacks fire until all jacks are charged. At that time, each port simultaneously fires identical charges down each of its outgoing cords.
- No element may accept charge when it is already charged (e.g. a charged port that is waiting on its sibling ports cannot receive another port in the interim); attempting to send a charge to an already-charged element will queue the charge in the cord (like trains waiting to enter a station).
- The ordering of the pins (arrayed top to bottom straddling the left wall of the block) and the jacks (arrayed top to bottom straddling the right wall of the block) is fixed and meaningful - other elements 'know' how to reference the ports via their numerical order. All port elements (i.e. both pins and jacks) can optionally be provided with names by extending them into hexagons, providing room inside for a label; for elements that straddle the block boundary, the 'label' part should always sit wholly inside the block.
- All elements other than blocks must be hosted by (drawn inside) a block; no element other than a block can host (have drawn inside of them) other elements.
- A block's content (its guest elements and all of their connections and behaviours) is entirely and solely a consequence of that block's 'coputs' (lines from the environment that attach to the top of the block) - though here Eve jots down a suggestive "Nested blocks...?"

At this point, Eve breaks for a well-earned cup of tea, leaving her diagram on her desk - where it catches the eye of one of her more software-oriented peers.

# Modelish as a lens to understand computing

We have stressed many times that Eve knows nothing about Python specifically, and very little about computing generally. We ourselves know quite a lot about Python and possess, we hope, an adequate grounding in computing generally. From this vantage, it is apparent that Eve's approach will struggle to thoroughly capture what her Python script is doing; but we can also see certain ways in which Eve's diagram actually surpasses - and thus anticipates - the limitations of standard laboratory code, and - perhaps - even suggests alternative architectures that would be considered very advanced if Eve had encountered them in the context of a formal qualification in computer science.

In principle, what Eva's new schema describes is some kind of natively concurrent, function-oriented programming language. The main currency is 'values' of some kind - there is no sense of 'type' as yet, but in Eve's case we might imagine them to be numbers or immutable arrays of numbers. The 'cords' (lines joining the elements directionally) convey information about those values from place to place in the form of 'charges' (a value, or rather a reference to a value, viewed in light of having been 'freshly calculated'). The 'gates' (green triangles) accept these charges, together with the particular logic as allocated to them by name from their host block, and use these materials to produce a new value; finally, references to that value are packaged as 'charges' to be dispatched onwards via the cords to whatever other elements require them. We can picture the execution of the program as the movement of glowing charges along those cords, triggering elements on arrival and precipitating the diffusion of new charges, rushing like a wave through the block from left to right.

In a function-oriented paradigm, a system like this could be thought of as a complex of functions (the gates) - specifically, what are called 'thunks', which are partial functions (functions with their input fields already pointed somewhere) where no fields remain 'open', with the consequence that an ennary function (a function of some number of variables) becomes a nullary function (a function with no variables). Thunks are conceptually and implementationally convenient because they can be substituted for the value that will eventually result from calling them, allowing us to act on a value before it actually exists. In this case, the block is instantiated by hooking up the pins and is executed by identifying the 'terminal thunks' (those gates whose only outgoing cords connect with the jacks) and evaluating them first. In practice, the wave of execution goes first in the opposite direction to what intuition might suggest: from right to left, until the inputs are reached, then back up from left to right, as the actual values are obtained and passed forward.

[Diagram: a simple mathematical expression represented in conventional maths notation as well as in Modelish form, illustrating the correspondence between a task graph and a complex of nested function invocations.]

Eve did well to intuit all this without knowing anything about 'paradigms' or 'nullary functions'. What she doesn't appreciate yet is that her lab script is written in Python: an interpreted, largely non-concurrent, dynamically typed, object-oriented language, very different from what Modelish describes 'naturally'. In some ways, Eve's diagram presents an algorithm that is more elegant and performant than what the script most likely implements. If we were to represent the lab script exactly in Modelish, it would look something like this:

[Diagram: a Modelish diagram with several inputs entering on the left and immediately being bundled together in to the base of a single gate; a single cord extends from the gate to the base of another gate, and from the point of that gate to the base of another gate, and so on; at the right wall, a penultimate gate shoots cords out to the bases of several different gates, one cord per gate, with each terminal gate connected to a single 'jack' (outbound port).]

The values on the gate cords (the green lines) represent the total state of the system: the complete memory footprint of the whole program, sans any immutable dependencies. The block contains one gate for every Python interpreter invocation during the entire run; loops and conditionals are serialised (potentially infinitely) with irrelevant branches scheduled but neutered by special runtime tokens in the global state.

The 'true' diagram is so flat because, in Python, it is impossible to totally sandbox any part of the code: there is always a pure-Python means of altering any part of the runtime from any other part. This very deliberate choice on the part of Python's developers - which, to be clear, has many desirable consequences, in addition to the obvious downsides - is partly behind the other reason why the diagram is flat: most Python implementations import a 'Global Interpreter Lock' that ensures that only one part of the program is running at any given time. Without such strict serialisation, two processes could attempt to alter the same memory simultaneously and crash the program, or worse.

If Modelish has proven so effective for representing Eve's use case, what Eve probably *really* wants is a type-safe, natively parallel (or at least non-concurrent) language in a function-oriented paradigm; something like Fortran, ironically; perhaps Elixir, if Eve wants to keep up with the Joneses. But declaring that Eve is simply using the wrong tool is missing the big picture: the issue is that Eve doesn't *know* what she needs. A laboratory that is struggling to upskill its students in Python is not going to fare better with Clojure; what's more, there's no guarantee that either Python or Clojure is going to be appropriate for the *next* task the lab chooses to take on. By the time Haskell enters the frame, a laboratory that was meant to specialise in landslide hazard modelling or viral resequencing has transformed into an ersatz computer science department with all the trimmings.

This would be another natural juncture where it would be appropriate to consider what artificial intelligence can offer. If Eve is able to comprehend what she needs to do and can express it in natural language, why not let the 'rubber duck' do all the typing? The principle stumbling block of this approach is that LLMs are tuned to deliver on expertly-written specifications: when prompted with vague or incomplete directions, they have a tendency to stray into what you might call 'patronisation spirals', where the statistical correlation of inarticulacy with incompetence leads to bad code nucleating on bad code. This is one factor behind the observation that the demonstrated productivity gains from AI - where they can be substantiated at all - appear to be nonlinearly positively dependent on user skill: the 'can-dos' can do more, and the 'can-nots' are served vapid rehashings of internet pseudocode that match the tenor of their own inexpert babblings. (Clever multi-agentic frameworks can mitigate this pathology, but if Eve is struggling with Python, demanding that she master OpenClaw may be a bridge too far.)

It is true that, with her Modelish diagram in hand, Eve has taken a big step towards being able to articulate her use case precisely enough that a skilled coder (or code agent) *could* successfully implement it. The question is not so much about whether it is a good idea to relinquish agency to a bot at this point, but rather: if just a little bit of formalisation helped Eve anticipate features like type safety and concurrency, what could a *little bit more* formalisation do? We will eventually demonstrate that the point of drop-off of the marginal utility of learning verges so close to the pick-up point of well-designed common methodologies that the gap between, where full robotic agency might prove useful, is often too small to justify the cost of task-switching, even in a deeply AI-forward work environment.

For now, our counsel to Eve (returning from her tea break) is to develop Modelish further, building out the conceptual toolkit while steadfastly remaining close by her specific use case and its underlying subject matter. The strategy is simple: to develop this intuitive, albeit abstract, formalisation to the point where the 'right implementation' becomes obvious, even to a relative novice. If we can leap out and catch Eve at the exact moment where her conceptualisation of her needs is at its fullest, we can leap straight from brilliant ideas to sublime programs and deliver "science at the speed of thought".

# The shunt element

Let's imagine that Eve decides to dive a little deeper into her lab script. She quickly finds that there is more going on than she first thought. There are loops and conditionals, nested functions, modules and submodules, and even some 'classes' - whatever those are. She also recognises that there is a big difference between the way that information moves between parts of the Python environment (by passing 'variables' around by name) and the way information moves in and out of the Python environment altogether (e.g. by means of reading and writing files, or parsing and emitting shell streams).

As a true Kuhnian at heart, Eve commits herself to defending her intuition at all costs.

Eve's first clever idea is to add a new kind of port: the 'shunt'. The shunt - represented with a yellow diamond, like its kin - is a port that can go *inside* a block, in contrast to the pins and jacks which are restricted to the walls. The shunt proves immediately useful for visually simplifying long-distance connections: if a gate produces charges destined for several other elements that are far away from it, the charge can be conveyed through a single cord and split at a shunt closer to where the charges are actually needed. This also makes the branching behaviour explicit: the ability to 'duplicate charges'  now emerges as a unique feature of ports. Eve likes this so much that she amends her earlier ruleset:

- Gates may no longer have multiple outgoing cords; instead, each gate must have exactly one outgoing cord.
- If a charge needs to be duplicated for dispatch to multiple consumers, this should be done with a 'valve' element (an internal port).

Eve now wonders if there is even more that ports can do for Modelish. If ports are the only elements that are allowed to 'emit' multiple cords from a single point (on the right side), what would happen if ports were additionally endowed with the ability to 'receive' multiple coords at the opposite point (on the left side)? How would this behaviour differ from the multiple inputs that a gate can receive?

Eve reasons from geometry. While the flat base of the gate allows the inputs to be individually distinguished, the singular point at which a port's ingoing coords meet makes them relatively indistinguishable - 'at the point', on a microscopic scale, they are all 'one thing'. Eve also reasons logically. If only gates are allowed to produce new values, a port has no way to arbitrate multiple different values to decide on a single 'true' value to propagate through to its dependants.

Putting these two intuitions together, Eve realises that a port's multiple inputs are a bit like an 'or' statement: the port can be triggered on *any* of its ingoing cords, which it forwards without modification through its outgoing cords (duplicating the charge if necessary). In the odd event that a port receives charges from multiple sources at the exact same time, it simply queues them up in anticlockwise order (effectively, from top to bottom). Eve pictures the charges flying along the ingoing coords and stacking up where they meet the left point of the port, taking turns to enter the port and be catapulted to distant climes.

The addition of the shunt expands the range of modellable behaviours dramatically. The very first thing Eve thinks to try looks something like this:

[Diagram: a Modelish block with one pin, one shunt, and one gate; a cord connects the pin to the shunt, another connects the shunt to the gate, and a third connects the gate back to the shunt.]

Eve sees that any charge entering via the pin is going to be caught in an infinite loop, passing through the shunt to the gate, being modified in some way by the gate, and then passing from the gate back to the shunt, and thence to the gate again. If the charge carries an integer and the gate increments integers by one, the block becomes a simple counter, ticking up to infinity.

Intrigued but satisfied with the new element for now, Eve turns her attention to another nagging matter. It is apparent, even to a non-expert, that a Modelish diagram resembles a kind of graph (an expert would identify it as a directed graph; potentially an directed acylic graph, if there are no shunt-loops). It is eminently possible to start with a single charge on one side and end up with many charges on the other side; it is also evident - assuming the diagram is compliant with the rules - that every cord will be traversed at least once in the course of executing the block, and every charge that gets produced in one place will end up being used in some other place.

Eve's laboratory script, by contrast, clearly has 'branching' logic in it: certain things that only happen if certain variables are above a certain value, say, or logic that gets applied differently if a value is a string or a number (an expert would call this a 'multiple dispatch' or 'polymorphic' system). Consequently, there are branches of the script that never get explored - not so with Modelish. But implementing a feature like Python's if-else blocks seems complicated.

Eve mulls this over while filling her bottle at the tap. It occurs to her that a sequence of conditionals is a bit like a sequence of taps: if one is turned on, another tap down the line might lose pressure; if the first is turned off, and the second is on, the water will flow there instead of here.

Eve rushes back to her desk and sketches out an ordinary shunt element, but with two extra lines: a 'handle' coming in at the top and a 'drain' coming out at the bottom. A shunt with a handle holds its charge until the handle itself is charged. At that time, one of two things happens. If the handles charge evaluates to the special 'None' token, the charge passes through normally. (Eve sets this convention with the reasoning that a shunt with no handle has in a sense been receiving 'nothing' through the top all along; thus it makes sense for the left-to-right flow to be preserved for this case.) If the handle's charge is anything other than 'None', the charge gets redirected out through the drain instead. Eve thinks ahead to the question of what the significance of multiple handle and drain lines should be and further decides to treat multiple handles in the same way as multiple inputs and multiple drains in the same way as multiple outputs.

The new-and-improved shunt element elevates the schema dramatically. With looping and conditional logic both placed within the bailiwick of a single omnibus element type, Modelish supports the complete core inventory of a practical programming language while distinguishing explicitly between control flow logic and 'permutative logic' (logic that alters or creates data). Eve is satisfied to discover that the schema now also doubles as a flow chart specification - handy!

# Blocks as generators

Imagine Eve adds a jack to her diagram:

[Diagram: as before, but now the loop also has a branch that connects to a jack (a right-side port).]

By the rules already established, the loop must dispatch a charge to this jack with each cycle. What becomes of that charge? What will happen if new charges arrive at that port in the future? If blocks are like big functions (with lots of little functions inside), what sort of function is this, that can 'return' a value more than once?

Eve's software-loving peer would immediately identify this as a 'generator' in Python; more generally, a 'coroutine'. It is yet another moderately advanced concept in computer science that Eve's intuitive approach has acquired without formal study. Rather than 'returning' or 'evaluating to' a certain value when called, a generator is more like a physical machine on an assembly line, carrying a mutable internal state that is inaccessible to outsiders, which it uses to alternately receive and produce information on an ongoing basis. Generators could be thought of as a superset of conventional functions: anything a function can do, a generator can do, but generators can do a great deal that normal functions cannot. If Eve embraces this escalation, her schema will become much more powerful - but it is will require a substantial intuitive leap.

Eve gets there in step. First, she recalls the rule already established that pins and jacks fire as sets: that is, no pin will fire until all pins are charged (at which point they all fire), and likewise for the jacks. However, this is as yet no specification of any synchronisation between inputs and outputs. It is at present entirely possible for the environment to trigger the block multiple times while it is still working through an earlier iteration. Not only will this cause charges to 'pile up'; it also threatens to befoul any internal logic that assumed that any charges in circulation are pertinent to a particular call, and not any subsequent ones. In particular, if the block contains an infinite loop, as Eve's working diagram does, subsequent calls after the first one will fluctuate randomly with the block's own self-triggering, interleaving in a pattern determined by the latency of computer hardware instead of in a 'natural, deterministic way - and what's more, with no external means of determining whether the value ultimately read off the jack belongs to a unique invocation of the block or one of an ever-growing legacy of 'echoes' from the past. (Eve's software-loving peer will observe here that Eve has organically stumbled upon the concept of the 'race condition'.)

Eve's makes two decisive calls.

Firstly, Eve decrees that the inputs and the outputs must henceforth be *synchronised*: the inputs will be prevented from discharging until the outputs associated with the previous input set have fully discharged. Attempts to trigger a block that is still 'running' will lead to charges 'queueing up' on the pins: the block will be expected to deal with each completed input set in the order in which it arrives.

Eve's second decree is equally impactful: henceforth, loops inside the body of a single block will be forbidden. Eve reasons that this is the only way to prevent a situation where different parts of the program 'race' each other, giving rise to 'unnatural' behaviours. The consequence is that every block becomes a true 'DAG' - a 'directed acyclic graph'.

The loss of looping functionality would seem to undo the progress made by the introduction of the shunt, but there is a solution to this: support looping, but only *across the block as a whole*. This will require the introduction of a new body element, two new boundary elements, and new low-level concept: the 'epoch.

First, Eve imagines splitting a port into two triangles. She glues the (rightward-facing base of the) left triangle to the inside of the block's right wall and the (leftward-facing base of the) right side of the triangle to the inside of the block's left wall. Assuming the two halves can still be identified as 'one port', the implication is that signals flowing into one half emerges from the other half - but, as implied by the new synchronisation rules, 'delayed' until the next time the pins are (collectively) triggered - which itself can only happen after the jacks are (collectively) triggered.

This strange new element - with two parts situated in two different places - Eve dubs a 'latch'. The half that gets attached to the right wall she dubs a 'semijack' and its mate a 'semipin'. The latch allows information from the previous firing of a block to be available in the next firing, even when the inside of the block is completely clear of charge (as it logically must be if the jacks have been fired and there are no 'internal' loops).

This new device implies something of a paradox. If a block is prevented from firing until *all* its pins are charged, but some of those pins rely on values from previous firings, how can a block ever be fired 'the first time'? Clearly, there must be some way to 'kickstart' a block - actually several ways. One way would be to allow semipins to specify a 'fallback' value for themselves; but this would require a whole new logic system, parallel to the usual system, and most likely redundant (as in, needlessly duplicative). A better approach is to allow blocks to 'boot' themselves up from the inside out. This can be done with a single new element: the red oval 'fuse' element.

The fuse element's sole function is to generate an arbitrary charge, exactly once: at the moment the block is instantiated. A fuse can be thought of as generating the 'working fluid' that will subsequently circulate through the block. At the time when the fuses discharge, no charge is forthcoming from any of the fuses: the whole input set is void. In conjunction with the branching capabilities of shunt elements, this allows the behaviour of a block's 'initial firing' to be quite different from subsequent cycles, if desired. Whatever the logic, eventually the charges will culminate at the jacks; any semijacks will have a chance to pump their mated semipins, and the block is primed to fire again as soon as values for the rest of the fuses are provided. 

If we imagine the initial firing establishing a counter, we can assign a natural number to every firing of the block. Following Pythonic conventions, we should imagine this counter being set to zero at block instantiation and incremented by one at the beginning of every subsequent firing. This counter is called the 'epoch'. Thus everything that occurs during the initial firing happens in 'epoch zero'; subsequent firings unfold in 'epoch one', 'epoch two', and so forth. The epoch value at any given time can be read as the answer to the question "how many complete input sets has the block received since it was instantiated'?

With the addition of these features, Modelish blocks are brought into much closer alignment with classic Python generators. 'Epoch zero' represents the execution of the function block up to the first 'yield' statement. Subsequent epochs label the action of the generator between yield statements. We have not as yet introduced any mechanism akin to the 'return' statement, however; that comes later.

# A general model of computation

Eve's journey into computer science, which gave us (a first draft of) Modelish, was predicated on a fairly common and intuitive model of computation in which the basic units are 'functions' of some kind and the basic action is the consumption of inputs and the production of outputs. That the functional paradigm *is* so intuitive to most humans (and, as it happens, AIs) is a good reason to build our models in that way. That is far from the only consideration, however. There are other paradigms that may be just as intuitive - even more so, if broached in the correct way. In any case, there is no particular reason to believe that what is intuitive is necessarily 'best', for any given criterion other than intuitiveness itself.

In fact, we have already seen our functional bias come unstuck when we recognised Modelish 'gates' as similar to classical 'thunks'. To recap, a thunk can be thought of as a nullary function which 'promises' a value that hasn't been calculated yet:

$$
f(\cdot) = 2 + 3 \\
g(\cdot) = 3 \cdot 5 \\
h(\cdot) = f() + g()
$$

In the above example, we can see how it doesn't really matter when, exactly, $f$ or $g$ gets calculated. Because they are nullary functions, we are certain that they will resolve to a certain value eventually. By the laws of arithmetic, we can even be confident of what 'kind of thing' will be produced - in all cases, an integer. Even if instead of $3 \cdot 5$ we had had $3 / 5$, we would still be able to guarantee that the values 'eventually' produced would be rational numbers at least. That is the basis of our confidence that we can invoke $f() + g()$ and not produce a syntax error. No matter how complex we make the arithmetic of $f$, $g$, and $h$, we can be absolutely certain that $h()$ will resolve to a single, legitimate numerical value that is at least rational, if not simpler, and that that value - $20$ - will be the only value $h$ could ever resolve to. With that being the case, it would seem that $h$ is a perfectly adequate stand-in for the number $20$ wherever it occurs.

If we can effectively beggar a function of its outputs, can we do the same with its inputs? As a matter of fact, we can, and not only is the procedure not exotic: it is foundational to the field of elementary arithmetic.

Consider:

$$
f(x) = x^2 - 5x + 6
$$

The inertia of a lifetime of conventional pedagogy would drive us to view $x$ as a 'variable' and $f$ as a kind of machine that eats values of $x$ and produces values of $y$. Until we 'pass in' an appropriate $x$ value, it is as if $f$ exists in its own parallel universe: it is a 'function' kind-of-thing, and not a number, and it cannot do the kinds of things that numbers do.

Yet there *is* in fact a special number associated with $f$, as schoolhouse algebra shows - more than one, actually:

$$
0 = x^2 - 5x + 6 //
(x - 2)(x-3) = 0 //
x = 2, x = 3
$$

The fact that we conventionally perform the root-finding process as a series of steps with 'statements' (all those equals signs) separated across multiple lines, this is really an implementation detail. We could imagine an alternate notation where the $=0$ part is simply implied:

[Illustration: a new notation called the 'cartouche' resembling a rectangle with semicircles for the left and right walls, wrapping the expression $x^2 - 5x + 6$]

The 'cartouche' wrapping the expression is essentially a notational device that initiates a dialogue with the free variables in the contained expression and outwardly expresses the value "whatever $x$ needs to be to make this expression equal to zero". The result can be viewed as a set, if we want to make sets the basis of analysis, or of a choice, if we want to take a procedural view. In either case, we have succeeded in reinterpreting a function of more than zero arguments as the 'promise of a value', just as with our 'thunks' earlier. The concept of the 'function' has now been beggared of both its inputs and its outputs.

If we ride this conceptual train to the end of the line, we arrive in a marvellous realm populated by many esoteric creatures that would be unfamiliar to most practicing scientists: lambda calculus, type theory, formal grammar, abstract algebra, and many others. What these fields all have in common is a general preoccupation with 'expressions' - things we can write down that 'mean' something, as opposed to 'doing' something - and the idea that complex expressions may be construed to 'mean the same' as simpler ones. Often, such systems have at their base a set of (typically very simple) forms that are viewed in a certain context as 'canonical': the form that is 'really real' in that context.

In this unfamiliar (though hardly untrammeled) realm of intuition, objects like 'thunks' and 'cartouches' can be viewed simply as expressions that are 'on their way' to some anticipated 'final form'. Instead of thinking in terms of functional 'maths machines' grabbing numbers and bashing them together, we have the more parsimonious notion of 'construction' - the building of complex expressions from simpler ones by means of some simple inventory of 'constructors' - and of 'reduction' - taking a complex expression and transmuting it into a simpler one. These two operations may be named and articulately differently in different contexts, but the basic rhythm of construct-reduce can nevertheless be found in the deep calculus of most, and perhaps all, information systems.

Since we are working across multiple fields, we cannot simply borrow some pre-existing systematics without inheriting an obligation to work within constraints that may not be appropriate in all cases. We could instead retreat to the philosophical literature, where the abstract issue of 'things' and the relations between them are dealt with in fields like mereology and object-oriented ontology - but that would drag us way off course. Fortunately, we have the option of sidestepping the prior art and building up our own framework without great difficulty; the notations and nomenclatures we introduce will be easy to relate back to their various field-specific correlates down the track.

# The abstract entity model

The abstract entity model is a conceptual and notational system intended to provide a simple, accessible, and universal framework for discussions of objects and their relationships. It neither explicitly develops on, nor aspires to supplant, any other framework covering the territory, but has been developed out of pure necessity as an aid for the modelling sciences. It is presented here because it underpins much of the larger work of the thesis in some way or other, and because of a presumption that it may prove as useful to others as it has proven to the author.

First, we introduce some (hopefully unobjectionable) basic nomenclature. Let an 'entity' be any discrete 'thing' in the domain of discourse. In maths, these might be numbers or operators. In chemistry, these might be atoms or molecules. An entity becomes an entity by being uniquely identifiable. Thus, in arithmetic, $2+3$ and $5$ would be considered different entities, even if we imagine them to 'mean' the same thing. Entities only interact with entities, and everything in the domain of discourse that ultimately relates to the subject at hand should be conceived of as an entity. For example, in arithmetic, the $+$ symbol is obviously not a number - but it *is* an entity.

If the only permitted objects in scope are all entities, where do the entities themselves come from? Logically, they must either be constructed from other entities, or have a prior and sufficient existence in themselves. In other fields, these twinned concepts have names like "composites and primitives" or "compounds and simples" or "molecules and atoms" or "phrases and words". Such nomenclatures either carry inappropriate semantic baggage (is a chemical 'molecule' really like a 'phrase'?) or are confusingly generic or adjectival (is there a difference between calling something 'primitive' and dubbing it 'a primitive'?). Instead, we should name these two important 'types of entity' in a way that linguistically references the parent type while avoiding any unintended references. Constructed entities shall henceforth be 'complities' and non-constructed entities shall be 'simplities'.

In most fields, 'complities' are assembled according to complex syntactic rules. For example, in arithmetic, new expressions are produced from pre-existing expressions by means of operators injected before, after, over, under, inside, or around other expressions. For our new, more generic framework, we shall make do with exactly one kind of 'constructor': let each complity be a flat, unordered collection of other entities. (Collections of this kind - effectively 'sets with duplicates'  or 'multisets' - are sometimes called 'bags' in computer science; thus a complity could be said to be simply a 'bag of entities'.) The collection that defines a complity will be called its 'content', and an individual element of the content set shall be dubbed a 'component' of the complity.

Eventually, we will provide the means to construct complities implicitly - that is, programmatically). Without such means, we are restricted to constructing them *explicitly* by wrapping angle brackets around (references to) the chosen components;  i.e. $\langle ... \rangle$, where the ellipsis should be replaced with a space-delimited sequence of other entities. Since the content set is unordered, the order of the components during explicit construction is completely arbitrary; later we will suggest a 'canonical ordering', with consequences for computer applications.

Now that we have a way to 'write down' an entity, we need to be careful not to 'confuse the map with the territory'. It may matter a great deal whether an entity is 'accessed' via a textual representation or, as we shall see later, via a visual representation, or something else altogether. When an entity is represented textually on the page like this - or in any other way - it may be referred to as a 'gram', and the representation itself an 'epigram'. This allows to differentiate between the thing, the thing as referenced in a certain way, and the reference in itself. For example, we can say "strip the brackets off the epigram", but we can't say "strip the brackets off the gram" (because the gram may depend on those brackets to retain its integrity), and we certainly can't say "strip the brackets off the entity (because entities do not have brackets)". This may seem excessive, but specificity permits precision, and precision is the name of the game.

Given we lack any higher-order syntax that would allows us to imply components *without* enumerating them (as in conventional sequences like $0, 1, 2, \ldots$), it follows that the only complities we are able to represent within the system at this time have content sets that are *finite* (though potentially arbitrarily large, depending on the material limits of the imagined 'page' upon which we write). This is programmatically equivalent to requiring valid construction algorithms to 'halt' at some point; if we want to maintain a correspondence between implict and explicit complities (so that anything that can be constructed implicitly can be constructed explicitly and vice versa), we will have to take care to restrict the construction language to those that are less than Turing complete. The same limitations also imply that there are no 'cyclical' entities: that is, there are no entities that are ultimately dependent in some way on themselves. This is crucial and distinguishes the abstract entity model from other mereological frameworks.

Unlike complities, simplities 'always exist' and cannot be constructed. This creates a little problem: we can reference a complity (by constructing it), but we have no way to reference a simplity. Conventionally, the simplities within a given discourse must be introduced and referenced by a symbol of some kind, but that introduces its own complications: what kind of 'thing' is a symbol; if it is an entity, how can it 'connect' to a simplity without forming a complity of its own? The study of symbols and signs (semiotics) is its own weighty branch of philosophy, and we do not have the luxury of engaging with it right now. Instead, we will refer to these special referencing symbols simply as 'noums', with the general etymological connotation of 'name' (and some other philosophical overtones that will be expounded later). Notationally, when it becomes necessary to refer to a generic simplity directly on the page without any fuss, we will use the first seven lowercase Greek letters as our 'noums' - $\alpha \ \beta \ \gamma \ \delta \ \epsilon \ \zeta \ \theta$.

At this point, we have enough notation to write arbitrary 'entities' onto the page, like thus:

$$
\langle \alpha \beta \langle \gamma \langle \delta \epsilon \rangle \zeta \rangle \theta \rangle
$$

Identical content produces identical complities, regardless of the order:

$$
\langle \alpha \beta \rangle = \langle \beta \alpha \rangle
$$

Since complities are based on multisets ('bags'), duplicates still count as unique components:

$$
\langle \alpha \alpha \alpha \rangle \neq \langle \alpha \rangle
$$

In cases where there may be lots of duplicates, we will allow the duplicates to be implied with exponential notation:

$$
\langle \alpha ^ 5 \rangle = \langle \alpha ^ 3 \alpha ^2 \rangle = \langle \alpha \alpha \alpha \alpha \alpha \rangle
$$

Consistent with that notation, the exponent $0$ should be read as "this entity is not a component of the complity":

$$
\langle \alpha^2 \beta^1 \gamma^0 \rangle = \langle \alpha \alpha \beta \rangle
$$

This will come in handy later.

Logically, a complity must either be composed of simplities, other complities, or a mix of the two. We can use this to assign a 'generation' number to each entity. Simplities are considered to be 'generation zero'. A complity made entirely of simplities belongs to 'generation one'; a complity with at least one first-generation component is 'generation two'; and so on. In general, a complity has the generation number of its highest-generation component plus one. Retroactively, this allows us to define simplities more directly as 'zeroth generation entities' and complities, by exclusion, 'non-simplities'. Amongst other things, the organisation of entities into generations implies that cyclical entities are impossible, since it would be impossible to assign a generation number to an entity that is required to be 'one greater' than itself.

We are yet to grapple with one puzzling possibility: a complity with no components - $\langle \rangle$. By our new definition, that would seem to make them simplities. Yet, unlike simplities, the entity $\langle \rangle$ exists regardless of the scope of discourse, as a kind of implication of the framework itself. Clearly it is a very special object.

If every complity is defined from a set, then $\langle \rangle$ is the complity constructed from the empty set, $\emptyset$. According to the rules of our entity system, there is exactly one such entity, and it is an entity that by definition must be shared between every domain of discourse - in other words, it is the one item that is truly universal. Far from being 'null' (empty, absent, vacant), this strange entity has a certain sense of being independently solid and present.

Let us dub this entity 'sol' - notated $\odot$. We shall henceforth require it to be notated such, and not indirectly via $\langle \rangle$, in order to free up that syntax for other uses down the track. 'Sol' is evidently not a simplity, since it has a content set, and it is equally evidently not a complity, since it is not of generation one or higher. It would seem that sol belongs in some special category of its own - a 'special entity' - though, for reasons that will shortly become clear, it must implicitly be of 'generation zero' (which in any case makes a certain intuitive sense, since 'gen zero' comprises the entities that are 'always already' there).

Now that the idea of an 'entity with an empty content set' has been absorbed by 'sol', we are left with a certain conundrum regarding complities proper. If a complity is defined by its generation, and its generation is always calculated by a simple formula over the generations of its components, then a complity with no components is a contradiction in terms. It seems inelegant (and potentially formally problematic) to simply force complities to always have components; what's more, this would seem to threaten the versatility of the entity model as a representational tool, since we can easily imagine countless real-world scenarios in which a 'null entity' is a natural notion (simple arithmetic, for starters).

If sol is truly primordial (generation zero) and universal (present in every context), it is also in a sense 'semantically blank' (since semantics attach to content, and sol is without content by definition). Thus a natural way to construct a 'null complity' might be something like this:

$$
\langle \odot \rangle
$$

When dealing with conventional sets, it is customary to view the null set as a subset of all sets - so $\emptyset \subset {2, 3, 4}$, for example. If the null complity contains $\odot$, and complities are 'like sets', it follows that all complities should be equipped with content sets that are supersets of the ${\odot}$ - which implies that $\odot$ is a component of every complity.

Such a construal is formally very powerful. For one thing, it starkly differentiates the nature of complities (which always have at least sol as a component) from the nature of sol itself (which is imagined as having a content set, but with no components inside). Speaking in terms of 'type', it allows us to emphatically lift complities into a different 'plane of existence' from sol, creating a formal and philosophical grounds to endow complities with traits lacking from sol, and to discriminate between complities and sol formally. (For example, later we will show how sol gives us a way to construct complities programmatically without brackets of any kind, retroactively putting the brackets themselves on a sounder formal footing.

The new interpretation of sol also allows us to revisit an abandoned piece of syntax from earlier. When we introduced the notation '$\odot$', we explicitly forbade representing it as $\langle\rangle$. We have the opportunity now to repurpose that notation as a representation of the 'null complity'. This is equivalent to saying that angle brackets always imply the presence of sol, and therefore that all such enclosures necessarily represent complities. This completely closes the syntactic space afforded by the angle brackets, with beneficial consequences for formalisation and programmatisation.

Let us turn our attention to the simplities now. Thus far, we have referenced simplities obliquely using Greek letters - $\alpha$, $\beta$, etc. The implication is that these are something like variables, in that they have a 'suprasyntactic' - their nature is owed to something outside of the entity system altogether. This is not an altogether satisfying treatment for such fundamental objects.

We can look for inspiration to the field of elementary arithmetic, which also has its 'ideals' - the prime numbers.

The fundamental theorem of arithmetic states that every integer greater than $1$ is either a prime number or a 'composite' - a product of primes. Since multiplication is both associative and commutative (i.e. $abc = cba = bac$ etc), the fundamental theorem implies that every number apart from zero can be represented as a multiset (a set permitting duplicates) of primes - e.g. $ 6 = \prod \left\{ 2, 3 \right\} = 2 \cdot 3 $. Multisets are necessary because there may be repeat factors - that is, factors that are higher powers of primes: $ 12 = \prod \left\{ 2, 2, 3 \right\} = 2^2 \cdot 3 $. If we allow the empty set, we can actually represent $1$ as well, since the product of no numbers is implicitly the multiplicative identity: $ 1 = \prod \left\{ \right\} $. (In a sense, the $1$ is 'always there'). Thus the fundamental theorem can itself be restated as "Every integer greater than zero can be represented as the product of a multiset of primes."

The similarities between this perspective on arithmetic and the construction of our complities are manifest and manifold. Evidently, sol ($\odot$) is analogous to the identity $1$, while the simplities play the role of the primes: we could declare that $ \alpha\sim2 $, $ \beta$ \sim $3 $, and so on. If the we then view the action of 'taking the complity' as analogous to 'taking the product', the first generation complities (those whose only components are the zeroth generation entities i.e. sol and the simplities) implicitly represent all the integers from zero up, starting with $ \langle \rangle \sim 1 $ and continuing through $ \langle \alpha \rangle \sim 2 $, $ \langle \beta \rangle \sim 3 $, $ \langle \alpha \alpha \rangle \sim 4 $, $ \langle \gamma \rangle \sim 5 $, $ \langle \alpha \beta \rangle \sim 6 $, and so on. Since every composite number has a unique prime factorisation, the mapping between first-generation complities and the natural numbers is safe and total.

Identifying $\odot$ as being 'like $1$' raises the prospect of having multiple sol entities in a single complity; after all, there are infinitely many implicit $1$s in any multiplicative expression. We could simply declare that complitisation is invariant under repeated $\odot$, but that is selling ourselves short. Certainly, a complity needs at least one $\odot$ in order to be a complity - we have just now ruled as such. That however does not exclude the possibility that there could be 'multiple instances' of a given complity, identical in every way but distinguished by having different numbers of sol components. We won't dwell on this possiblity right now, but it should be noted: it will prove useful down the track.

The analogy between elementary arithmetic and the entity model suggests many useful extensions to our notation and formalisation. For one, the new analogy implies that there are infinitely many simplities available 'by default'. Primes are typically represented abstractly using the notation $p_n$, where $n$ is a natural number greater than zero: $p_1=2$, $p_2=3$, $p_3=5$, etc. We can borrow this convention if we ever get tired of using Greek letters (or if we simply run out at some point) - effectively extending our vocabulary of 'noums', or symbols that reference simplities.

On the topic of notational convenience, we might take inspiration from arithmetic's use of natural exponents to represent 'repetition' - e.g. $ 12 = 2^2 \cdot 3 = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 $. We could mirror that in our own notation: $ \langle \alpha \alpha \rangle = \langle \alpha^2 \rangle $. We could even extend the notation over non-simple components: $\langle \langle \alpha \rangle ^ 2 = \langle \langle \alpha \rangle \langle \alpha \rangle $. It is not hard to foresee scenarios where this could compress an entity representation dramatically.

Another notion we can borrow is the idea of ordering the complities. We will cover the question of order at greater length in just a moment. For now, it seems self-evident that first-generation complities, at least, can be naturally ordered by their 'arithmetic value': so $ \langle \alpha \beta \rangle \gt \langle \beta \rangle \gt \langle \alpha \rangle $. This (not coincidentally) has some nice analogues with how directed graphs are conventionally ordered.

If we choose to view non-zero positive integers as literally (multi)sets of primes (rather than as merely equivalent to them), then there is a sense in which the number system has two versions of every prime: for a given prime $p$ there is $p$ itself, but there is also $\left\{p\right\}$. There is a certain power in viewing it that way, with the primes (the 'ideals') being 'prior', and the concept of 'number' emerging as a new layer over the top. In the same way, we have the zeroth-generation entity $\alpha$ and the first-generation entity $\langle\alpha\rangle$. Both in a certain sense 'mean' the same thing, but it is important to the broader purpose of the entity model that they be considered different - however related. There is a certain sense in which $\langle\alpha\rangle$ is different - 'greater', even.

Of course, our system goes further still. We have even higher-generation complities: second, third, fourth, and more, without limit. What's more, a complity does not necessarily have components of all one generation: from the second generation onwards, there are complities that might have some simplities, some first-generation entities, some second-generation entities, and so on. We would love to inject all these entities into the number line somehow - there would seem to be plenty of space.

This relates to the idea of a general 'canonical ordering' for entities. It is tempting to use the numerical values of the less-than-second-generation entities as the basis for such an ordering, imagining that the spaces between the integers can somehow be 'filled up' with higher-generation entities. This, however, assumes that the cardinality of the entities is that of the real numbers, when it could easily be much greater: without providing more constraints on the methods of construction, we cannot say at present. While not abandoning the idea of a general 'arithmetisation' of the entity model entirely, there is much we need to move through before we are ready for that.

A simpler approach for now is to adopt a 'lexicographical ordering', like the entries in a dictionary. First, we stipulate that each generation's entities are altogether greater than the entities of previous generations. Thus $\langle \alpha \rangle$ is greater than $\alpha$ and $\alpha$ is greater than $\odot$. Next, we stipulate that an entity with more components is greater than an entity of the same generation with fewer components (in the case of simplities, the same intuition applies if we view them as multisets of $\odot$; if viewed more strictly as powers of $\odot$, we can simply order them by exponent). Finally, for entities of the same generation that are also of the same length (but are *not* in fact the 'same entity'), we order their respective contents from greatest (left) to least (right) and compare the components in pairs, one from each entity: if equal, we go to the next pair, and then the next, confident that - sooner or later - we are sure to find an entity that breaks the tie. Despite the complication of layering, this proves to be exactly analogous to comparing the words 'bus' and 'but' in a dictionary and is served by the same extremely quick algorithms. When taken in view of the impossibility of constructing cycical entities, this ordering also implies the very useful property that entities are always 'greater than' their components, since any component of generation $n$ implies a complity of generation $n+1$ which is by definition greater.

# The orbital entity diagram

How are we to picture these 'entities'? Insofar as entities are like sets, we might be include to picture ellipses on a page with elements (potentially sub-ellipses) floating inside. This is not a tenable visual model of the entity system we propose, since in our system there is no such thing as 'duplicate entities'; every entity is unique, and an attempt to create a 'new' entity identical to another entity simply creates a new reference to the same entity. This foregrounds the notion of 'references' as being first-order objects of the system.

Clearly we must be envisioning a system of nodes connected with arrows - that is, a graph. Furthermore, it is apparent that no node may support a forward connection to any node that it itself ultimately depends on - otherwise, it would be impossible to maintain a consistent numbering of 'generations'. This kind of structure is conventionally called a directed acyclic graph (DAG).

A DAG representation of a system of entities would begin with sol ($\odot$) as the 'root node': the 'sun' and the source of everything else we might draw.

Unless we are happy drawing only a single null complity, and nothing else, we are going to need simplities to start building anything of real interest. Where do these entities sit, graphically speaking? What do they look like?

So far, we have always represented them symbolically with our so-called 'noums' (e.g. $\alpha$, $\beta$, and so on). If the symbols are fairly compressed, representing the simplities symbolically may often be more concise than attempting to depict them graphically, and so one approach is simply to allow the textual and graphical forms of the entity model to be blended and allow noums to be written directly onto the page as references to the simplities, wherever or whatever they are. This principle of 'textual-graphical fusion' is one that we will try to adhere to in all our notations.

If we are adamant that the simplities should be represented purely graphically - perhaps because we haven't actually assigned any symbols yet and we aren't happy with the default ones - we could exploit the fact that the simplities (whatever they represent) always come in a certain order (because they are analogous to the prime numbers). In that case, we might augment the sol icon to be a kind of 'wheel' with little flanges coming out of it, starting at the twelve o'clock position and continuing clockwise. Each flange represents a simplity, in order, up to whatever number of simplities we choose to accept in our system. To make a particular simplity a component of another entity, we simply connect to the appropriate flange to indicate a connection to that simplity. (Later, we will see how this notion of the simplities being 'inside sol' in a certain sense is quite apt.) We declare that there may only be one 'sol wheel' on any given 'page', and that the presence of that icon unambiguously signals that what is being depicted on this page is an entity chart.

Now we have a basic vocabulary of simplities to work with, we can get start on the complities. A simple dot represents the new entity. At birth, the new entity is by definition a 'null complity' (or rather, *the* null complity): it has no components apart from sol, and sol is implied, so that connection needn't be specified. If we want to denote a less trivial complity, we need to bring in the components somehow. We do that with simple arrows, originating at the entity that is to be the component and terminating with an arrowhead at the dot representing the new entity. In the case of the first generation, all such lines will originate from either one of the flanges on the sol wheel, or equivalently, with a noum written on the page that references the simplity directly. Later-generation complities can be represented in the same manner as the first generation, except that some or all of the connecting arrows will now be sourced from other complities, instead of from simplities.

Complities, of course, can have multiple 'counts' of each component. We could represent this directly by having multiple arrows reach out from the component and attach to the new entity in different places - but if the new entity is being represented as a small dot, this could easily get very confusing. A better approach is to represent the count on the arrow directly, either indirectly with a stylistic device (a heavier weighting on the line, for instance), or directly with a small number (e.g. $2$ for two counts); if the latter, we should establish a clear policy and require the number to be both 'upright' on the page and written to the left of the arrow, close to the arrowhead, from the perspective of an observer standing on the line facing the same way as the arrowhead.

The fact that entities exist in a succession of generations implies that these 'arrows' actually don't have to be arrows at all - that is, they don't need to have arrowheads on them, because the directionality is implied by the relative generational membership of the two participants. This might seem iffy: surely we can imagine two dots drawn side by side with a line joining them, leaving it completely ambiguous which connects to which? Recall, however, that every complity bar null has one or more ancestors, and those ancestors are always 'closer to sol' than oneself. The two dots we just imagined are strictly illegal, because neither is connected to sol. Once one or other bears such a connection, the directionality is self-evident. (The only exception is a relation where one of the parties is the null complity, since this has no explicit connection to anything (its connection to sol is implied, not drawn); the null complity, however, can be represented directly using the special symbol $null$.)

Since an arrow with no arrowhead is not really an arrow, we ought to coin a new term: we shall call such a connecting line a 'cord', evoking electrical cords which likewise have a directionality to them that is natural but not necessarily indicated. Depending on how the nodes are laid out, it might still be helpful to grant cords some sort of directionality cue (e.g. an arrowhead), but as we'll see in just a moment, there ways to lay things out so that even this is unnecessary.

Such a diagram could quickly grow unwieldy. If we know in advance how many entities we have in each generation - or alternatively, if we have the ability to shuffle things around - we might think of a slightly unconventional visualisation strategy and place sol right in the middle of the page, with the various entities 'orbiting' in concentric rings, one for each generation. If the radius of each 'orbit' is calculated as a function of the number of entities in that generation, we can ensure that there is always enough room along the circumference to host every entity without crowding, optimising the use of space without compromising readability. Now that the generations are strictly laid out in radial order, we can discard any lingering directionality cues on our cords: since an entity can only depend on lower-generation (thus further-in) entities, directionality always flows radially outward, and is trivial to identify regardless of any angular tendencies.

This 'orbital entity chart' can be further enhance by applying the lexicographic ordering we described earlier. The canonical ordering is already partially applied by virtue of the layout of the rings in order of increasing generation. The next step is to order the entities within each ring in the manner of a clock, starting at the twelve o'clock position and placing each entity so that a later place 'in time' corresponds to a higher priority in the ordering. This is helpful in itself in that provides a canonical visual representation to match the canonical textual representation. There are advantages for readability too, however, due to the way that the canonical ordering reflects the underlying flow of dependencies. If each orbit starting from the first generation is rotated so that its highest priority entity shares the same angular coordinate as that entity's highest-priority component (which will necessarily be somewhere on the next orbit in), the orbits will tend to synchronise for many topologies and the angular deflection of cords can be minimised.

A few geometric tricks can improve the visual layout still further. If the cords are themselves required to depart and connect radially (i.e. going directly radially outward from the source node and directly radially inward to its destination node), and approach a purely angular tendency at the halfway point, they will consistently form elegant S-shaped curves that emphasise the succession of generations; this has the added benefit of bringing similarly-tending lines into more or less parallel courses, like nerve bundles, further minimising criss-crossing and drawing attention to larger patterns of dependency.

Preventing lines from inadvertently crossing over or behind nodes is also vital; the 'sigmoid' rule partially ensures this (since even very long cords will politely stick to the inter-orbital space for most of their trajectory rather than taking a shortcut across the middle of the diagram), but we can additionally ensure this with a slight change to the layout of the nodes themselves. If a node of an earlier generations serves a direct dependant in a generation that is multiple orbits removed from its own, the cord will have to traverse those interposed orbits and may intersect with one or more of those orbits' nodes along the way: at worst, this could spuriously imply a connection where there is none. We can prevent this by tracking which nodes have 'deep dependants' and reserving for them a place in each interposed orbit where deeply outbound lines can safely pass through. Ideally, these 'ghost entities' would be directly radial from the true entity, but whether they are or not, the sigmoidal rule should still be adhered to, ensuring that all lines flow outward in an orderly manner with minimal kinks and clashes.

There are a few more stylistic and formatting choices we can make that pay dividends for readability and general visual appeal. The 'weight numbers' on each line that tell how many 'counts' of that entity the dependant needs are visual clutter most of the time. They are also hard to place without either breaking up the rotationaly symmetry (by remaining upright for the viewer) or readability (by rotating with the diagram and thus appearing upside down in places). These values could instead be represented by varying line thickness. This could be calculated on a logarithmic scale, so that differences between one and two references to an entity are stark but differences between eleven and twelve references are less imposing. The nodes themselves, which were originally just dots, could also be expanded into generously sized, flat-toned discs, forcing the orbits that host them to be a bit bigger and thus make more room for the cords to splay out and disambiguate with each other.

Colour and shade also remain at our disposal. We should refrain from making any claims on colour at this time (since the user, or we ourselves, might have need of that syntactic dimension in the future), but a little shading can make a big impact to readability. Select a shading gradient - not too wide - and have every node select a shade at random to be their own. Now have every cord flowing out from that node adopt the same shade. The variation can be very slight and the impact is still emphatic, as several classes of jarring optical effects are disrupted at once. This strategy also enhances the semantics of the diagram, breaking up the spurious symmetry of repeating nodes and emphasising their individuality and unique patterns of descent. If the background is also shaded, in a gradient from lightest around sol to dimmest at the outermost orbit, the effect is enhanced and the diagram as a whole becomes much more navigable. Shading the diagram this way also improves the visual storytelling, accentuating the core operant metaphors of 'sol' and its increasingly remote worlds.

# The tabular entity chart

There are times when human readability is top priority; at other times, it is important to compromise between machine and human intuitions, both for the sake of the machine, and to aid the human in thinking like a machine, which is essential if one is to drive them effectively.

When we introduced the exponential notation for components, we briefly noted that an exponent of zero could be read as "not a component" i.e. a notation that explicitly excludes an entity as a component where it might otherwise be assumed to be present. Another consequence of this choice which we deliberately did not emphasise at the time is that it implies that every entity is in a sense 'actually' connected to every other entity, even if some of those connections are 'valued' zero.

This is in fact a common way of thinking about graphs because it allows them to be transposed into tables, which are both highly expressive and highly performant in a computational sense (at the tradeoff of being very wasteful in memory). Take a graph of $n$ entities and draw a table of $n \times n$ dimensions. Every entity is now represented as both a row and a column. When a entity appears as a column, that column has a slot for every other entity (including itself); the value in each slot is the exponent value that would appear over that entity in the standard bracket notation, defaulting to zero if the entity is 'not a component' (for example, the slot representing the column entity itself would host a value of zero).

The real advantage of the tabular format is apparent when we look at the rows. Here, for each entity, we see an orderly registry of all forward dependants. This is massively helpful for navigating the graph: without it, every step out from a particular entity would require us to check every entity of generation $>n$, which is explosively costly for large graphs. In a computational sense, being able to see immediately whether a node has any dependants allows us to quickly check if it is an orphan or not. We could even place summation boxes at the end of each row and column: the end-column summations gives us the 'net weight' of that entity (its total number of dependencies), while the end-row summations give us the 'net dependence' of that entity (the total number of dependants). When the net dependence is zero, the entity is an orphan and could be carved away without affecting the rest of the graph at all. This is essential for memory management as it allows redundant objects to be easily and safely 'garbage-collected'.

As with our orbital entity chart, this tabular form can be enhanced with a few simple measures. Firstly, of course, we should order the entities lexicographically, and identically on both axes (such that the self-references are exactly on the diagonal). Ideally, we would maintain the order at all times by inserting each entity in the right place when it is created: this is a simple matter when the table is already well-ordered. The table can be enhanced for humans and machines by flagging (for a human, shading) the generations, which will form neat blocks of columns and rows when the table is correctly ordered. With a careful choice of shading, the intersections of the generation blocks row- and column-wise can be made obvious even deep within the table. Room should also be left in the shading gradient for a standard alternating pattern in both directions, which allows us to abandon the hard lines that tables usually have without sacrificing the ability to visually move along single rows and columns. Finally, the numerical values in each cell can be replaced with flat discs, with the area of the disc scaling with the value in the cell. This makes 'heavier' dependencies feel visually weightier and allows the table to be read easily even when it is huge (and the cells thus very small).

The final transformation we should make is obvious once the elements are properly ordered. Since no entity may be dependant on entities in its own generation or higher, most of the cells around the diagonal and below should be empty. If we step back and view the table as an intersection of rows and columns of generations (rather than individual entities), it is clear that what we actually need is a pyramid-shaped table (technically a triangle, but a pyramid is more evocative), where each tier of the pyramid represents a generation of entities (starting from sol at the top) and each slab of the tier from left to right hosts information about that generation's dependencies on one of the earlier generations. Such a layout is not only more expressive from a human standpoint, but also more efficient from a machine standpoint.

# Evolving entities

Thus far, the entities we have described have been purely inert: semantic objects that might be viewed as representing something, but not programmatic objects that can be 'acted on'. In reality, many of the sorts of things we'd like to represent in an entity-like manner are in fact subject to change. Molecules assemble and break up under different potentials; organisms are born, live, and die; battles are won and lost. While there is value in being able to represent a static state of affairs, the real world is dynamic.

One of the motivating cases for the entity model was the example of mathematical expressions. If we wrap a 'cartouche' (a rectangle with rounded left and right walls) around an expression in one or more variables - indicating a desire to know the 'roots' (the values of those variables at which the expression is synonymous with zero) - we create an object that in a sense 'wants' to transform into something else. That new object may itself be a cartouched expression, awaiting its own onward transformation. This sort of behaviour, where it is encountered in most fields, is called - amongst other things - 'evolution'. In this case, it is as if the laws of arithmetic themselves define an evolutionary force, driving objects within from one configuration to another.

Let $\star$ (pronounced 'unpack') be an unary operator that consumes an entity in a given environment and returns another entity in the same environment. The nature of the environment determines the behaviour of the operator: the same entity transposed into a different environment might be affected by unpacking very differently.

We can visualise this by introducing dotted lines or 'rays' to the diagrammatic language of entities. A ray connects one entity to another, expressing "this entity wants to evolve into this other entity". Since there is a directionality here that cannot be simply inferred from context, we will have to include arrowheads on our rays for now - later on, we will introduce features that make this redundant.

Now we can create a field of entities and imply some evolutionary force simply by drawing rays between them, from entity 'foo' to entity 'bah' and from entity 'bah' to entity 'qux'. We can even draw multiple rays connecting to the same entity from different places: there are plenty of ways to evolve a cat.

What happens if we draw multiple rays *from* a given entity? What could that imply? From the example of our cartouches, perhaps such a pattern indicates multiple equivalent 'solutions' to the same unpacking operation. In the same way that the expression $(x-2)(x-3)$ has valid roots for both $x = 2$ and $x = 3$, so may a single entity in any given environment be impelled to evolve in two different directions at once. How we manage this is up to us. We can put our thumb on fate's scale and choose one of the branches to the exclusion of the others; or, we can branch the universe into multiple parallel histories, one for each possibility. Thus interactivity and concurrency are both implied and supported, even in this very simple system.

In principle, the introduction of rays partitions the entities into four essential kinds. We will call them 'Ev types' (short for 'evolution'). 'Ev1' and 'Ev3' (the odd types) cover those entities with no rays emanating from them. Such entities are deadends, and unpacking them represents a choice of no options, or equivalently, a branching into no futures: the end of evolution and the termination of the 'program'. 'Ev2' and 'Ev4' (the even types) are the complement: entities which *do* have forward emanations and thus can be unpacked to move the system forward. That covers the outbound rays: now for the inbound rays. 'Ev1' and 'Ev2' together comprise the entities which have no inbound rays at all - that is, they cannot be reached by unpacking another entity, but can only be obtained by constructing them directly. By contrast, entities of 'Ev3' and 'Ev4' types (the second two types) always have one or more rays connecting to them, allowing them to be reached by evolution. The relative abundances of different 'Ev types' in a population of entities characterises the 'evolutionary graph' that relates them.

One can easily create evolutionary graphs with any mix of one or more of these types. The entity graphs we were drawing before we introduced the concept of evolution and rays can retroactively been seen as Ev1 graphs: every entity was an isolate under evolution, and none could be unpacked to reach any other, nor reached from any other by unpacking. At the opposite end of the spectrum, one could imagine a closed loop of entities, where 'a' evolves to 'b', which evolves 'c', which evolves to 'a'. Such a graph is pure Ev4.

The introduction of Ev2 and Ev3 entities makes the graph somewhat more complicated. An Ev2 node - which can be unpacked, but cannot be reached by unpacking - is conceptually a kind of 'start node': it is the furthest back that the spring can be wound before evolutionary forces take over. An Ev3 node is the opposite: a 'stop node', which - when reached - halts the process of evolution altogether (or at least, the thread of evolutionary history that led to it); in short, a dead end. Logically, no graph can be made of purely Ev2 nodes or purely Ev3 nodes, since the existence of an Ev2 node implies at least one Ev4 or Ev3 node, and the existence of an Ev3 node implies at least one Ev4 or Ev2 node. In many non-trivial graphs, most nodes will be Ev4, some will be Ev2 or Ev3, and none will be Ev1.

So we can draw up our entities any way we want, and we can connect them with rays any way we want. That is fine as far as it goes, but it's really just drawing pictures. If, however, we step back and consent to allow our hands to be tied by a little logic, we can do much more. Imagine an arbitrary entity chart with rays drawn across it. We could take those rays and represent them instead as a table of rules: "If you are at an entity with one $x$ component and one $y$ component, go to the entity defined by having one $y$ component and one $z$ component." At the top, we might think to simply list all the Ev2 nodes (if any): "Start at one of 'foo', 'bah', or 'qux'." And at the bottom, we might add a terminating condition for any Ev3 nodes we have: "If you reach one of 'zap', 'vim', or 'lop', stop."

It is obvious that what we have really written here is a computer program: most likely a very tight and explicit program, but a program nonetheless. If we study our list of rules closely, we might actually find some simplifications we can apply: perhaps it transpires that we always voyage to entities with $y$ after reaching entities with $x$, and the other conditions on the relevant transitions are redundant. In that case, we can strip these overspecific rules from the table without changing the evolutionary graph one jot. If, however, we then choose to add a *new* entity, we are no longer free to simply draw rays to and from that entity at will (at least, not without creating some new rules). Instead, the place of that entity in the evolutionary graph is dictated by the rules we have specified: if the entity has a component $x$, the onward voyage to entities of $y$ is implied and required. Because the rules are now more general than they were before, it is entirely possible that a newly introduced entity will enter the graph in a rich and highly connected way, though it was totally out of scope before.

So if it is this table of rules that ultimately 'joins the dots' on our entity chart, where does the table come from? Where is it stored? Is it an entity, or is it something else?

On one level, it's not necessary to address those questions: the entity model is already doing something useful as it is. If we are scientists studying some particular phenomenon, where various discrete entities seem to appear, disappear, and transform over time as a function of the prior population of entities, then simply drawing the entities and tracing the rays of descent will begin to tell us something right away. If we continue the exercise along the lines we've laid out, we end up with a table - itself a kind of model - which makes predictions about how newly emergent entities might be expected to behave. If those predictions are falsified, we default back to drawing rays directly and updating the table to describe the rays; then when we're feeling more confident, we switch once again to 'predictive mode', where the table does the talking, and see where the implications lie.

If, however, we want to situate our model in the context of something larger and more complex, we have to take very seriously this question of what the 'table' is and where it comes from. It is a property of its environment - that we know - and it influences the entities in its scope without being influenced by them, which makes it 'greater' in some sense. If we want to fold this idea of a 'table of rules' and an 'implied evolutionary graph' into the entity model itself, we need to find a way to put entities back in the driver's seat.

# Sub-Entities and the Recursive Entity Chart

Let us revisit the basic entity chart we had at the beginning. There are little dots and connecting lines ('cords') joining them up. What happens if we expand each dot into a circle? Now the cords connect at the circumference, instead of meeting up at a single point. This is more legible, but it is also suggestive. What if the circle is hollow? What if it is quite big? Does the enclosed whitespace not then suggest a certain affordance? This is the first step in transforming a simple entity chart into the so-called Recursive Entity Chart.

The Recursive Entity Chart implies a new kind of entity - the sub-entity - and a new kind of binary asymmetrical relation: the relation of enclosure. A sub-entity, put simply, is an entity 'drawn inside' another entity - specifically, in the newly available whitespace we just created. The visual object - the hollow circular shape enclosing the 'body' of the entity - shall henceforth be dubbed a 'cell': thus the REC can be thought of as a system of cells with cords flowing through them.

Though we have approached this new idea from a visual language standpoint, in truth it is an extension of the Abstract Entity Model itself - and a powerful one at that.

First, recall this notation for entities, which we introduced earlier:

$$
\langle \alpha \beta \langle \gamma \delta \epsilon \rangle \theta \rangle
$$

An entity represented in this way, if we will recall, is referred to as a 'gram' (and the notation itself, an 'epigram').

We will now introduce an extension to this notation, using the pipe character $\vert$ (`|` on a computer). The pipe character goes inside the angle brackets. To the right of the pipe go the usual gram contents ($\alpha$, $\beta$, etc.). On the left of the pipe, we expect a 'gram' (a reference to an entity). That gram denotes the 'parent' of the entity: the entity that the new entity - the 'child' - is to be 'drawn inside of'. An entity's parentage is as fundamental to its identity as its components: two entities can have completely identical components, but if they have different parents, they are nevertheless *different* entities. A gram with no pipe is assumed to be 'all right side', with the left side unwritten but implied - a tricky notion, as we shall see in just a moment.

First, consider:

$$
\langle \langle \alpha \beta \rangle \vert \gamma \delta \langle \theta \rangle \rangle
$$

Here, the gram $ \langle \alpha \beta \rangle $ denotes the parent entity, in which we are to imagine drawing an entity with cords bringing in components $\gamma$, $\delta$, and $\langle \theta \rangle$.

Note that no parentage has been provided for $\langle \theta \rangle$ - nor, for that matter, for $\gamma$ or $\delta$. As a matter of fact, no parentage was provided for the parent either. Where exactly does $\langle \alpha \beta \rangle$ live? Surely not *inside* its own child? We could require the parentage of literally every component to be flagged, on both sides of the pipe - but that would not only clutter the notation intolerably, but inevitably lead to an infinite regress: are we to imagine some 'ultimate parent' that puts a stop to it?

Let us declare that $ \langle \alpha \rangle $ is synonymous with $ \langle \vert \alpha \rangle $ - in other words, the pipe and the 'parentage section' it denotes is always there, but may be empty. When there is nothing to the left of the pipe, we default to a system of 'implied parentage'.

The first step is the most abstract. When representing entities using epigrams or charts, the 'ultimate parent' is the page itself. That implies that the page is an entity: we may indeed think of it as such, or rather, as the whitespace inside of the entity when drawn as a node in a chart. The borders are nowhere to be seen, but somewhere beyond our vision, they are there - with a world of entities beyond our comprehension lingering on the other side. This may seem like a somewhat baroque or even absurd abstraction, but as a mnemonic, it supports exactly the right intuition about how entity representations are to be treated in any given context (even in a computing context, as we shall later see).

If we 'draw' an entity on the page (creating a 'cell'), it naturally becomes a child of the 'page entity' ('global cell') itself. Likewise, when we note down an epigram with no pipe (as we did as a matter of course until recently), the presumptive parent is likewise the page entity (the 'global gram'). Thus the gram $ \langle \alpha \rangle $ is a 'child of here', wherever here is.

Inside an epigram, the rules are different. The space to the left of the pipe (if one is present) is considered to be 'outside of the gram' as far as implied parentage is concerned. That means that the gram $ \langle \alpha \rangle $ and the parent of the gram $ \langle \langle \alpha \rangle \vert \beta \rangle $ are exactly and literally the same entity. Conversely, the space to the *right* of the pipe (or equivalently the whole body of the gram, if no pipe is present) is considered to be literally 'inside' the parent, whether that parent is given explicitly or implicity. So for example, with the gram $ \langle \langle \alpha \rangle \vert \beta \langle \gamma \rangle \rangle $, both the gram itself and the component gram $ \langle \gamma \rangle $ are implied to be children of $ \langle \alpha \rangle $. This brings into alignment the visual notation (where the entity 'dot' is enlarged into a circle with 'space inside') and the epigrammatic notation (where the angle-bracketed enclosure after the pipe is the 'space inside'). Retroactively, one can look at the 'page entity' itself as being represented by an epigram, with the whitespace before you right now being merely an arbitrary swathe of its post-pipe section.

With the syntax now established, we can do something that wasn't possible before: we can pull a cord *across* the cell wall (though only in one direction at present). Consider this:

$$
\langle \langle \alpha \rangle \vert \beta \rangle
$$

This denotes an entity in the page context with a single component: the entity $ \langle \beta \rangle $ which is itself in the context of the global entity $ \langle \alpha \rangle $. Since $ \langle \alpha \rangle $ is by definition a sibling of the new entity, the $\beta$-based object that is ultimately being made the component can only be connected to the entity by extending a cord from inside $ \langle \alpha \rangle $, out, and across its boundary.

There is nothing forbidding the transgression of cords across entity boundaries, and indeed, it is a desired behaviour. It may however clash visually with the cord connections that denote the entity's own component. In a quick sketch where the flow of dependencies is obvious, this can be neglected, but where precision and neatness is required, a little care can improve readability markedly.

Firstly, let us declare that component cords must connect to the target entity with a little 'knot': a small sold dot, attached to the inside of the cell wall at the point where the cord makes contact.

Secondly, let us declare that traversing cords must request a little 'window' in the entity wall, which can be represented by a slight fading or lightening of the wall's tone at that point - or just an empty gap, if the continuity of the wall is in any case obvious. As an alternative for situations where such stylistic variation is not feasible, we will allow a 'bridge' iconography, where a pair of breves sit on the traversing cord at the point of traversal, mirroring each other such that one is concave in the clockwise direction around the cell's circumference while the other is concave in the anticlockwise direction: the traversing cord appears to travel across the 'bridge'. To distinguish outbound cords from inbound cords, the 'bridgeheads' for inbound heads could be rotated 90 degrees around the point of traversal such that they are concave in each direction along the trajectory of the cord: we could think of these as the mouths of a 'tunnel', with some cords 'tunneling' into target cells while others 'bridge' out of them.

Finally, standard onward cords (that connect an entity to those entities for which it is a component) may be highlighted witha 'tie' device: a small caret character attached at its vertex to the base of the onward cord, but sitting inside the source entity, like an arrowhead with no shaft 'pointing' out in the direction of the cord. (We will be giving a different but analogous meaning to this notation later on.)

We are yet to include in our new recursive framework the other kind of entity: the simplity. As per our custom, the various entities denoted here as $\alpha$, $\beta$, and so on have all been (presumptively) simplities. Do they have parents? If so, is the parent the implied parent or some other entity somewhere? If not, doesn't that rather challenge the notion of a recursive entity system?

We already ruled that simplities, like 'sol' ($\odot$), are zeroth-generation entities. Thus the question of where simplities belong is really a question of where sol belongs. Does it transcend the recursive system, or is there a sol 'inside' every entity?

There is only one good answer to this question, and it becomes evident when we consider the nature of the null complity $\emptyset$. We carefully defined $\emptyset$ in all dialects so that, wherever we have a notation to construct complities, attempting to construct one without any components gives you the null complity. (For example, writing $\langle\rangle$ and drawing a closed circle with no in-cords both give return a reference to the null entity.)

Key to this objective was the determination that $\odot$ is a component of every complity - including $\emptyset$. Since any two complitisations that draw on the same components denote the same entity, if sol was global instead of local, every attempt to create the null entity in a particular place would instead point to a single, global entity. That would place null in the same category as sol, when the entire motive behind constructing it the way we did was to ensure that it was treated the same as any other complity.

If instead we characterise sol as a local phenomenon - with each complity containing its own version - the systems we have already established are not only protected, but enriched. Given that sol is a component of every complity, but is also local to a particular entity, we could retroactively rule that a complity's parentage is fully indicated by the parentage of its sol component. The parentage of sol itself could then be represented as a relationship with the enclosing entity's sol; thus the 'recursive' part of the recursive entity chart becomes a kind of 'sol network', with all other entities in a sense 'hanging off' this network.

Now we have decided where sol belongs, and given that simplities have already been established as being in a sense 'embedded in' sol (e.g. as flanges on the orbital entity chart), we can revisit the question about the parentage of simplities and answer directly: yes, simplities have parents; in any given context, the parentage of a simplity is determined in the same way as the parentage of any other element. Thus writing $\alpha$ inside an entity produces a reference not to some global object reference by $\alpha$, but to whatever $\alpha$ means in the local context.

We will see that this decision not only creates an extremely powerful platform for abstraction, but also deepens the recursive nature of the entire system: if sol, the simplities, and all other entities are 'always somewhere', then we never need to be concerned with the existence or otherwise of some 'ultimate layer': we can comfortably assumed that, whatever whitespace sits before us, it is always 'inside something', even if we can't see what it is.

Now that simplities are local phenomena, like complities, there are situations where we might wish to reference a particular entity's simplities outside of the context of that entity. We might be tempted to try to 'pull' the simplity out using the notation we already have - for example, to pull $\beta$ out of $\langle\alpha\rangle$, we might try:

$$
\langle \langle \langle \alpha \rangle \vert \beta \rangle \gamma \rangle
$$

This, however, would return the *complity* of $\beta$, not $\beta$ itself. We currently have no means of addressing an entity without building a complity around it.

If we want to support this kind of action, we need to introduce another new notation. We will use the forward slash character '$/$'. The slash character can be used in place of the pipe character to indicate that the thing to the right should be treated as a single object, not as a set of objects denoting the content set of a complity. Thus, to retrieve $\beta$ from $\langle\alpha\rangle$, we would simply write:

$$
\langle \langle \alpha \rangle / \beta \rangle
$$

Visually, this is exactly the same as physically reaching into $\langle\alpha\rangle$, connecting the pen to that entity's $\beta$, and drawing a cord out, across the entity boundary, and into local space (where one might eventually connect it in with something else).

As with the pipe notation, everything to the right of the slash is read as being 'inside' the given parent, whether that parent is implicit or explicit, while everything to the left indicates the parent itself. When there is nothing to the left of the slash (i.e. the parentage section is blank), we fall back on 'implied parentage'. Thus $\alpha$ and $\langle/\alpha$ are exactly synonymous, and $ \langle \langle \alpha \rangle \vert \beta \langle / \beta \rangle \rangle $ is actually synonymous with $ \langle \langle \alpha \rangle \vert \beta^2 \rangle $. (There *must*, however, be something to the right of the slash - since, otherwise, what is being returned?)

The slash can be used to pull out complities as well as simplities - e.g. $ \langle / \langle \alpha \rangle \rangle $ is actually the same as just $ \langle \alpha \rangle $. This creates the opportunity for a syntactic error if the writer attempts to put a simplity in the parentage section of another epigram: since simplities cannot have any children by definition, such a notation fails to return a valid entity. That is the price of acquiring this new capability.

It follows from the logic we've laid out so far that, in principle, the only entities that can be made components of a new entity must be descendants of that entity's parent, because even if we try to override the implied parentage of one of those components, sooner or later we end up with an implied parent, and the implied parent of a component gram is always the parent of the enclosing gram:

$$
\langle \langle \alpha \rangle \vert \langle \beta \vert \gamma \rangle \delta \rangle
$$

Here, even though we've given one of the components its own unique parent, that parent - $\beta$ - must itself have some kind of parent, and the only parent we can reach for is the parent of the gram as a whole ($ \langle \alpha \rangle $). Yet if we adopt graphical means, we can easily reach far beyond these constraints, drawing cords that traverse generations up *and* generations down in one long arc.

To extend the range of entities reachable in written form to cover those reachable in graphical form, we need the ability to move directly 'up' in parentage. We can introduce this using the same sort of notation we used to move up directories in many computer file systems: dot notation. One dot represents the 'local directory' (which is in a sense what is implied when the parentage is left blank). Two dots represents the implied parent's parent; three dots represents that entity's parent; and so on. The notation works the same whether it precedes a pipe or a slash.

Now we can do this sort of thing:

$$
\langle \langle \alpha \rangle \vert \langle .. / \beta \rangle \rangle
$$

This denotes a reference in the page context to a complity inside $ \langle \alpha \rangle $ whose sole component is the page-level simplity $\beta$.

In deeply nested structures, reaching all the way up to a shallow context might require a number of dots that challenges readability. In environments where the meaning is obvious, we will allow a special parent token, inspired by Linux: the 'home shortcut' $~$. In the context of this very paragraph, here and now, $~$ refers to the page upon which this text is written. Thus the entity we just represented could be equivalently notated as:

$$
\langle \langle \alpha \rangle \vert \langle ~ / \beta \rangle \rangle
$$

Now that we have introduced dot notation and even a 'home' shortcut, it should be apparent that we have been drifting all along towards a certain intuition: entities as computer files, and epigrams as effectively file paths. If we embrace that intuition, the logical next step is to support multiple-segment filepaths. If we extend the syntax of our recently introduced 'slash' notation, we can denote these in a way that effectively supports traditional forward slash-delimited POSIX paths.

Consider the following, rather unwieldy epigram:

$$
\langle \langle \langle \langle \alpha \rangle \vert \beta \rangle \vert \gamma \rangle \vert \delta \rangle
$$

That is a lot of characters to represent a fairly simple idea. It would be much cleaner if we could simplify that 'parentage section' in some way.

If we inspect the parentage section here more closely, we see that it is ultimately composed of a whole sequence of 'complitisation bodies' (groups of components). If we pull these out and put them one after another as 'segments' of a single, flat structure with some appropriate delimiter, we could get rid of a pair of angle brackets for each nested parent. If we extend '$/$' for this purpose, the above could instead look like this:

$$
\langle \langle \alpha \rangle / \beta / \gamma / \delta / \rangle
$$

This is effectively saying "Starting at the page context, go into the complity of $\alpha$, then go into the complity of $\beta$, then go into the complity of $\gamma$, then go into the complity of $\delta$, then return a reference to it." Conceptually, it is as if the first '$/$' functions as an alternate form of $\vert$ where not one, but multiple 'complity bodies' are permitted, each (including the last) terminated by another '$/$'. The thing ultimately returned at the end is a complity - the one indicated by the final segment - just as is promised by a standard '$\vert$' character. The trailing slash not only clarifies that the thing denoted is a complity, but brings our notation into alignment with typical filepaths, where a trailing slash (often optional) unambiguously indicates a 'directory'. 

In standard filepaths, the first segment (before the first slash character) typically has a special role as the 'root' for path evaluation, with each subsequent segment taking you one node deeper into the tree from that starting point. The analogy for this behaviour within our epigram notation would be to treat the first segment strictly as a 'parentage section', supporting all the notation, and only the notation, that the left side of a pipe-notated epigram does. For example, the leftmost slot could host 'special parent tokens' (e.g. $..$, $...$, $~$), but the later segments do not; conversely, the later segments permit multiple adjacent items (to denote the bodies of complities) but the first segment does not.

There are a few caveats to observe. In a traditional filepath, each segment after the first is treated more or less the same. Not so in our system, where the rules about implied parentage provide different semantics for each segment: the $\beta$ in the second segment of $ \langle \langle \alpha \rangle / \beta / \rangle $ refers to *local* $\beta$ (i.e. $\beta$ within $\langle\alpha\rangle$), not 'page $\beta$'. Another idiomatic feature is the possibility of 'empty segments' as in $ \langle \langle \alpha \rangle / / \beta / $; presumptively, these *are* permitted and explicitly represent the null complity in the active context (just as in $\langle\rangle$ or $ \langle \langle \alpha \rangle \vert \rangle $). Whether null entities can have children, and what that represents, will be discussed in another place.

While we are doing the work of aligning our notation with convention, we should cover the case where the leftmost segment is empty - e.g. $ \langle / \alpha \rangle $. Based on the rest of the notation we've outlined, the natural assumption to make here would be that this denotes parentage from (page) null. However, in a POSIX-type filepath, "/foo" would usually denote an 'absolute path' - that is, a path with respect to the filesystem boundary. In the current treatment, there is as yet no obvious correlate to such a notion, but there may be in the future; and in any case, we have other ways to specify null parentage. We shall decree that the case of a left-flush forward slash is reserved - for now.

The most obvious remaining unallocated swathe of syntactic space lies at opposite end of the path section: the space after the *rightmost* forward slash. We have explicitly forbidden this up to now, but in so doing, we have destroyed the functionality supported by '$/$' in the first place. Now that we have clearly established the extended syntax for forward slashes, the decent thing to do is to politely return the unused syntactic space to its original owner. We can do that without clashing with the new syntax since we previously stipulated that '$/$' demands an argument: when it lacks one, we know that the requested object is the parent itself.

With that final piece in place, we can write this kind of thing:

$$
\langle . / \alpha / \beta / \gamma \rangle
$$

The above instructs us to enter the complity of alpha, then the complity of beta within that, then grab a hold of the simplity associated with $\gamma$ and return a reference to it.

# Pointers, Grams, Cursors, and Anchors

It may have elicited some consternation that we went to great trouble to ensure that angle brackets only ever represent complities, then introduced new notation (with the '$/$' character and equivalents) that made simplities representable as epigrams. It may also have been noted that there has been some vagueness up until now about the actual relationship between complities, simplities, and 'grams', which we vaguely defined as "a way of accessing a complity".

At the root of this matter is the fact that we never really clarified what sort of relationship complities have with their components in the first place. There is an enormous difference between possessing a thing and possessing a reference to that thing. It is a consequential distinction mathematically and philosophically, but also formally, and especially, computationally. We've evaded the question by saying things like "$A$ contains a (reference to) $B$", but that implies that there is such a thing as a 'reference' and that it is in some sense a 'different thing' to the thing it references (the 'referent'). Yet all along we have stubbornly insisted that there is only one 'kind of thing' in this system - the 'entity' - leaving no room in our ontology for references as a 'thing-in-themselves'.

In many (relatively) lower-level programming languages, like C, the distinction between things and references is a first-order concern. In such contexts, a 'reference' viewed as 'its own thing' is often called a 'pointer', and is implemented as a hexadecimal number interpreted as an address in computer memory where the 'actual thing' is stored. To act on the 'thing itself', the pointer is traditionally 'de-referenced' first, as if it were a coupon to be exchanged for the referent.

In the system we expound here, the concept of the 'gram' has been deliberately held apart from the concept of the 'entity' precisely so that it might eventually fill this role. Completing this conceptual transition requires revisiting the fundamental nature of entities and introducing some new 'kinds of thing' along - with new notations to denote them.

First, consider the significance of the arrowhead on a conventional arrow. An object lurks at either end of the arrow: at the base, some sort of 'source', and at the tip of the arrowhead, some sort of 'target'. Now, if we imagine taking the arrowhead and sliding it down the shaft until it lies somewhere closer to the midpoint. What has been lost here, semantically? The relationship between source and target is still implied, just as it was before, but the arrowhead now seems to have its own independent existence. We could imagine moving the arrowhead up the page, away from both source and target, until it stands on its own, higher than either. We could even imagine drawing a line from the arrowhead itself - perhaps from a dot inside the body of the head - and extending that out to be an input to something else. The arrowhead now presents as something new: a special kind object that takes two other objects and brings them into some relation. The arrowhead objectifies the relation itself.

This arrowhead object, viewed as 'its own thing', is what you could call, literally, a 'pointer'. It is the 'type' of thing we've been representing all along with our colon notation. For example, this is a pointer:

$$
\langle \langle \alpha \rangle \colon \beta \rangle
$$

We might feel trapped in something of a *reductio ad infinitum*: if pointers are now objects in themselves, than surely we need a pointer to reference them, and then a pointer to reference that pointer, and so on. Where does the infinite regress stop? Traditionally, this would be left implied, but we can do better by introducing a special kind of pointer: a pointer whose target is in a sense 'us'. This we could call a 'cursor' - an on-screen element that tells us where the user interface is currently concentrated. We shall represent it with a cross on the entity chart, and as a traditional I-shaped element (after the 'held' object) when represented textually:

$$
\langle \alpha \colon \beta \rangle \cursor
$$

The above can therefore be viewed as directly manifesting a state of affairs in which the 'thing in hand' is the simplity $\beta$ within the (global) entity $\langle\alpha\rangle$. Where the presence of a cursor is obviously implied, we will allow it to be omitted, but henceforth it should always be viewed as extant, somewhere, even if invisible. If we write or draw a single entity on the page, the cursor will be presumed to be attached to that entity; we multiple entities are extant, it will be presumed to attach to all of them in some implicit order (how a pointer can attach to multiple sources will be discussed presently).

Now that the 'pointer' exists as a 'kind of thing' in our ontology, we can clarify what sort of relationship complities actually have with their components. Rather than baldly declaring that a complity 'has' the various entities that make up its nature, we can refound complities on a more robust footing: a complity is not a multiset of 'entities' (as such), but a multiset of *references to entities* - that is, *pointers*. All the properties of complities that we already expounded remain valid, except that an entity's components are not immediately present, but are only implied by the presence of the pointers and must be 'retrieved' before being identified. For example, the law of identity for complities still applies (that two constructions denote the same complity if they draw on the same components in the same abundances in the same context), even though the constructor itself only ever directly acts on pointers, not on entities as such.

Pointers are defined by their source. Whereas entities are all unique based on their construction, pointers are unconditionally unique: i.e. two pointers can share the exact same source, but remain separate objects. We will assume for now that pointers are immutable (that is, that the source is locked in from the moment of instantiation), but the possibility of mutable pointers may remain to be considered at another time.

Pointers, of course, also have a target, and since a pointer is meant to 'objectify the relation', it should in principle only have *one* target. If the target is in a sense 'owned' by the object, that changes the implied nature of the thing being targeted, which must then be a 'thing in itself' with some independent existence outside of the 'arrows' that point to it. Alternatively, a pointer could be construed as having a 'slot' for a target that gets 'used up' when another object exercises ownership over it. We won't worry too much about this distinction for now (it is in a sense an implementation detail), but the implication is that pointers should behave exactly like conventional (notational) arrows do: they go from $A$ to $B$ and do not 'branch' along the way. We will adhere to this in principle, while allowing some fuzziness in practice to permit less cluttered visualisations in situations where the unique identity of each pointer is not important. When one sees multiple cords emanating from a single pointer (i.e. multiple 'targets'), one could instead imagine multiple pointers with identical sources piled on top of each other, giving the impression of just one.

If complities are now restricted to be 'made up' of pointers, what are pointers 'made up' of? It is tempting to simply allow pointers to be sourced from anything we want: simplities, complities, or other pointers. This however risks creating a circular type dependency, where the entity type 'knows' about pointers, but pointers themselves 'know' about entities. This approach will cause formal problems down the track. Yet how can pointers function as intermediaries with other entities (as they must when we are constructing a complity from them) if they cannot directly connect to them?

This is the reason why we waited to introduce pointers until *after* we introduced the notion of recursive entities. Recursion - and the idea of parentage and the 'space inside' an entity - gives us a way to unambiguously interpret a pointer as a reference *to* something without allowing the pointer itself to know what it is referencing: we can simply place a pointer *inside* an entity, with nothing for its source, and view that as a proxy for the entity itself. Graphically, this creates the impression of a kind of 'inside-out' arrow, where the arrowhead is at the source end and the shaft extends from the tip of the arrowhead rather than from the base. This new kind of 'void-source pointer' we shall dub an 'anchor'.

Textually (and formally) we can view the 'anchor' as the object 'actually' retrieved via this familiar notation:

$$
\langle \alpha \rangle \equiv \langle \vert \alpha \rangle \equiv \langle . \vert \alpha \rangle
$$

When we first introduced this notation, we described it as a 'reference to an entity' (in this case, to the global complity of $\alpha$). We also called it a 'gram', while somewhat evasively insisting that a gram is different from an entity.

Now we are equipped with the concept of the 'anchor', we can make a more robust determination: the notation $ \langle \alpha \rangle $ manifests a reference to a new anchor inside the (global) complity of $\alpha$. A 'gram' is then just what we call an anchor when we obtain it by means of an epigram.

This new way of understanding what is 'actually' denoted by a standard epigram gives new sense to this sort of construction:

$$
\langle \langle \alpha \rangle \langle \beta \rangle \langle \gamma \rangle \rangle
$$

This denotes a complity of the (global) complities of $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\gamma$. Each angle-bracketed enclosure creates a brand new pointer - that is, a gram - and makes it available to the enclosing constructor to be included in the new complity.

Just as we introduced a new visual device for the 'cursor'-type pointer, we should introduce one for the 'anchor'-type pointer. We will use a small 'caret' character, drawn anywhere inside the body of a cell, with a cord extending forward from it to whatever the 'target' is. The caret points in the direction of the cord - just exactly absorbing the sensibility of the 'tie' convention we introduced earlier. If we like, we can imagine the two horns of the caret 'pinching' the body of the entity to allow the cord to subsequently 'drag' it. Each attempt to reference an entity simply creates a new anchor, represented as a new caret inside its body.

The new visual vocabulary has the neat side-effect of clearing up a visual ambiguity we declined to resolve earlier: now the only cords that connect directly to the circumference of a cell are those that ultimately indicate the denoted entity's components. All other cords traverse the boundary neatly (potentially across 'bridges' and 'tunnels' if preferred). If the component cords are unambiguously notated with solid dots at their heads (as suggested earlier), the notation can be cleaned up even further by moving the anchor all the way to the circumference (as if, like in tennis, the border is considered 'part of' the interior), clarifying the sense that an anchor is 'actually' a reference to the cell (and its corresponding entity) itself.

The same logic we just introduced for anchors, cells, and complities, allows us to close another conceptual gap we have left open until now: the distinction between simplities and what we have called 'noums'. Like grams, we introduced these fairly evasively as the 'means of referencing simplities'. Now we can elevate noums into first-order objects by recognising that they are 'actually' a kind of pointer. Like the 'void-source pointers' we dubbed 'anchors', noums have no source, but are conceptually 'children' of a simplity: the only sort of object a simplity is permitted to be the parent of. Textually, the symbol $\alpha$ - when 'read' - effectively 'instantiates' a new noum; visually, the flanges on the expanded 'sol' icon fill the same role, as do literal symbols written on the REC (e.g. the character $\alpha$ sitting within the circumference of a cell); since symbols have no obvious 'attachment points' for onward cords in and of themselves, we will allow such an attachment point to be denoted with a flat line somewhere in the vicinity of the symbol, perpendicular to the subsequent cord and situated at its base.

If we can ensure that simplities are obtainable only via noums, we can declare that complities are fully composed of pointers of one kind or other - as in:

$$
\langle \alpha \langle \beta \rangle \rangle
$$

Where '$\alpha$' returns a noum and $\langle \beta \rangle$ returns an anchor - both varieties of 'pointer'. In this manner we ensure that - logically, formally, and philosophically - the 'thing itself' is always kept apart from its secular representations, and that there really is 'actually' only one of every entity: for even when we try to construct the same entity twice, we always end up with merely two different pointers that happen to ultimately 'point at' the same thing.

# Verses

When we introduced the idea of the 'cursor', we briefly noted the implication that a pointer might have multiple 'sources'. It may also have been noted throughout that we have carefully adhered to a convention where arrowheads are always represented in closed 'wedge' forms rather than open 'barb' forms. These chunky 'tactile' arrowheads were the first objects we described as 'pointers', though - confusingly - we went on to treat the word 'pointer' more as a description of a type of thing than as a thing in itself.

We left all these loose ends dangling to prepare the ontological fabric for a final, very important, concept. We call it a 'verse', and it is what we have 'actually' been representing by our 'wedged arrowheads' thus far.

The wedged form of the verse symbol, with its pointy tip and capacious base, gestures at the nature of the 'verse'. The mandate to include a target (thus an outbound cord) for every pointer bar the 'cursor' unambiguously identifies the base as distinct from the other sides of the wedge. The base itself, with its flat breadth and two terminii, suggests a kind of sequence. The symbology suggests that the verse is a structure that accepts any number of inbound cords, but exactly one outbound one.

The cords entering at a wedge's base connect the denoted verse to its 'feet'. These have an implied order: when facing the same direction as the verse, the leftmost cord represents the 'first' (the 'most accessible') feet and the rightmost cord represents the 'last' (the 'least accessible') foot. When drawn facing to the right, as verses often will be, this convention ensures that the verses exhibit an intuitive ordering from top to bottom. When drawn facing up, a heap of verses often resembles a tree, and the 'leaves' (the feet of all the lowest verses) can then be represented in a straight line from left to right, like a parse tree in common  formal grammar notations.

Textually, we can represent the verse by adding a new symbol to our epigrammatic notation: the colon symbol '$\colon$'. The colon symbol works the same as the slash ('$/$') symbol, except that instead of implying either an anchor (zero feet to its right) or a reference to a particular thing in the denoted context (a single foot to its right), the colon can support any number of feet to produce a verse of those feet in that order in that place. For example, the sequence $\alpha\beta\gamma$ (in the page context) would be represented as:

$$
\langle \colon \alpha \beta \gamma \rangle
$$

This is exactly equivalent to a wedge drawn on the page with $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\gamma$ arrayed along the base - with $\alpha$ on top when the the wedge is facing to the right (which would put it on the left if the wedge was rotated to face upwards).

As with the slash character, the space to the left attaches to the parent in which the verse is to be constructed. The meaning is sufficiently unambiguous that we can happily combine the new colon notation with the old slash notation in the same epigram:

$$
\langle . / \alpha / \beta \colon \gamma \delta
$$

This would return the pair $\gamma\delta$ in the context of the complity of $\beta$ inside the (global) complity of $\alpha$.

Apart from its ability to have any number of 'sources', a verse is the same as any other kind of pointer. It has a parent (i.e. a context); it expects its source (or sources) to be other pointers; it is acceptable as a source for other pointers (i.e. other verses or cursors). Most importantly, a verse demands a (single, unique) target: a wedge with no onward cord is like a gram that is not written anywhere. (An object that is not 'held' by anyone is effectively non-existent;' even the cursor has a 'target' - that being we ourselves.)

Similar to a complity, a verse is a compound that ultimately breaks down into a system of 'atoms'. Since every pointer may have only one target, a verse represents the head of a tree, which branches down through its constituent verses (if any) until, sooner or later, alighting on a population of 'primitive pointers' (pointers without sources - i.e. anchors or noums). Since verses are explicitly ordered, and the tree only branches at (sub-)verses, the 'primitives' underpinning a given verse form a flat string - which could itself be constructed as a verse. The primitives to which a verse ultimately decomposes shall be dubbed its 'tails', and the verse that one might construct from them (in the same context as the original verse) shall be called the 'entailment' of that verse; it is the imaginary verse made up of all the primitive pointers that are ultimately 'entailed' by the verse in question. (Put more conventionally, an entailment is the 'recursive concatentation' of a verse.)

Since a verse can have any type of pointer (bar the cursor) as a source, we have a certain conundrum when it comes to the construction of complities. When a complity is built out of noums and anchors, its identity as a multiset of entities is intact - since after all, these pointers can always be 'dereferenced' to produce one entity each. With verses in the mix, the matter is confused.

One obvious solution would be to expand any verse used in complitisation by effectively 'spilling' its entailment into the complity body. That would take a structure like $ \langle \langle \colon \alpha \langle \colon \beta \rangle \rangle \ rangle $ and produce $ \langle \alpha \beta \rangle $. This effectively introduces a progammatic means for constructing a complity: if have access to a number of verses that contain useful 'pre-baked' sequence of entities, we can use those verses to construct complities with many implied components using just a few explicit components.

The sense of verses as a means to not just reference entities, but to *imply* them, is the gateway to a much more expansive vision for our entity model.

# Evolution in a recursive chart

Before we expanded our formalisation to embrace recursive entities, we briefly explored the idea of 'evolution' and the notion that entities could be 'sent' to other entities by means of 'rays' (dotted lines). We paused that discussion when it became clear that there was no way to bring the 'table logic' (the logic that dictates what 'goes to' where) inside the entity model itself. In other words, it was unclear where entities actually received the information that dictated their evolution.

Now that we have added recursion to the mix, we have a prospective answer to that question: perhaps an entity's evolution is dictated by its *parent* - that is, the entity that a particular entity is constructed 'in the context of'.

If the evolutionary graph is implied by a given entity, that means it must be implied by whatever makes that entity 'itself'. In the case of complities - the only entities that can have children in the framing we've offered thus far - there are exactly two sources of identity: its content and its parentage. (This is really only one source of identity, if sol is the marker of parentage, as we briefly discussed.) Thus the evolutionary graph is somehow an emergent property of a (multi)set of entities considered in a given context.

With evolutionary trajectories governed by parentage, we can effectively stipulate that each entity gets its own 'table of rules'. That implies that 'rays' - the elements that connect progenitors to progeny - are contained fully within a given entity, and thus that rays may never cross cell walls under any circumstances. The webwork of rays that defines how entities transmute into one another remains forever local to an entity: a fact about itself that is not the business of outsiders.

With the addition of pointers, and the sense that an entity exists if it is 'in hand', the notion of evolution - individuals changing through time - turns into something more like a transition of handholds: an agent 'grabbing' one entity after another. This is effectively how an object-oriented computer language works: the actual data manipulation happens somewhere out of sight, and what is made available to the control layer is a system of pointers, which are exchanged for one another in order to advance the program.

If we want to migrate towards this sensibility, we already possess the notational firepower: we have the 'verse' construct. Like cords, rays can be drawn from the nose of each verse to attach to the base of subsequent verses.

If we represent rays as dashed lines, the same verse icons can provide directional information for two independent graphs simultaneously: a 'construction graph' (solid lines) and a 'production graph' (dashed lines). As with the entities, the 'inputs' to each verse (interpreted in the context of the entity hosting that verse) define what how many rays emanate from it and to where. The production graph effectively implements the evolutionary graph: since entities can never actually be 'acquired', but only referenced, the evolution of the verses (or rather, the evolution of our possession of the verses) tracks the underlying evolutionary trajectories of the entities. Done correctly, verses can totally absorb the evolution syntax of the entities and free them up to be purely semantic in character.

We previously established that any given verse in any given context can be collapsed into a flat structure of pure entities (or rather, of pure void-source pointers). We call these elements the 'tails' of the verse and, as a collective, the verse's 'entailment' (that which it 'entails'). Each entailment can be viewed as a sequence of entities. If entities are viewed as constituents of an alphabet (a set of symbols), a sequence of entities is something like a 'string' - a chain of symbols with an emergent joint symbolism; and a system that tracks the evolution of strings begins to sound less like a representational system and more like a computational one: a program.

# The great whiteboard experiment

Let us now embark on another thought experiment: an exploration of what the act of computation looks like 'from the inside'.

Imagine a room full of whiteboards. Also imagine we have at hand the full set of (excellent) Staedtler markers in a dozen colours or more. It is axiomatic to computing that a sufficiently large 'buffer' and the means to arbitrarily inscribe and erase symbols is all that is necessary to 'reach' all valid states in all legal programs (a crude but not inaccurate summation of the Church-Turing thesis). That we hope eventually to pass off the inevitably repetitive labour to some machine does not absolve us of the need and obligation to understand what we are doing at this level of granularity.

We could of course just start 'doing the maths' - but that would foreclose the possiblity of automating the workflow in the future. Putting machines out of mind for a moment, we might pose ourselves a focusing question: how can we structure our task so that a team of grad students could do it together, without knowing anything about that team (its size, competencies, etc.) ahead of time? What's more, how can we coordinate our team so that the outcome is determined solely and absolutely by the nature of the task, and not by any irrelevant exigencies? (For example, if we are to trust the eventual results, we need to know that they would have come out the same even if a certain student taking a bathroom break halfway through a critical calculation.)

It is a problem we can tackle directly. If we take all the ink, in all its various colours, in all its various expressions on all the whiteboards in the room, we have a 'global state'. We would assert that this state must be complete, so that our grad students can be fired and replaced at any time (good for motivation). If we erase everything but the problem description (tucked into a single corner of a single board), we should imagine that we have completely reset our progress to step zero, regardless of who is in the room at any given time or what those people may recall or not recall about the past. (After all, the correct working must still be demonstrated even if we're already fairly sure we know where it will end up - no-one is infallible or perfectly trustworthy.)

Clearly, if our problem is in fact 'tractable' in the most general sense, there must be something that can be written on some part of some whiteboard that would make us say "Eureka!" - something that we, the instigator of the effort, would trivially recognise as 'an answer'. Establishing what this 'end token' should be is actually something of a challenge in itself. It could be as simple as the letters "QED" tucked away in some pre-determined corner of the room. That, however, would oblige us - a flesh-and-blood human - to consume the entire state of the room in order to actually 'acquire' the 'answer'.

Instead, we should give some thought to what we want the entire state to look like at the end, not just the part that claims to represent the 'output'. Purely 'working' ink ('scratch memory') should of course be erased. We may decree that any number of intermediate stages should be erased too, since it may not be important to us 'how we got here'. More likely, we are interested in that, but not to the extent that we are happy to store every bit in perpetuity. We need to store on that final board 'the answer', some proof that it is 'the answer', and some metadata that answers subsidiary questions like:

- What is the question that this answer claims to answer?
- What is the assumed context of the answer? (There is always some context!)
- What pathway did we take to obtain the answer?
- What shortcuts (if any) can we now see in hindsight that would make this answer less costly to obtain?
- What does a reviewer need to do to walk that pathway and confirm our reasoning for themselves?

We can see now that this 'object' that represents 'the answer', with all its collateral, could itself be enormous and complex: like an dictionary that needs an index, that needs a guide, that expects a degree in philology to read. If our original purpose was to resolve a certain 'problem', and this object purports to be 'the answer', we might say that have a new problem now: the 'answer problem'. Thus we could say that the end goal of a scientific inquiry is not 'an answer' to that inquiry, but a new inquiry, ideally easier to resolve than the one we started with, the resolving of which induces a state in the solver that "satisfies them as knowledge".

In a very large system, which is automated to some degree, we might expect some or even most of the interlocutors to be non-human entities - the mind rushes to artificial intelligence here, but strictly speaking, any 'information system' trusted to run without supervision counts. Such entities are arguably not capable of 'knowledge', but they still face something akin to the 'answer problem'. For example, a large database query could be viewed in terms of a program attempting to reproduce part of another program as a third program; that query could easily return much more data than a human can 'consume', and thus prompt us to make various attempts to summarise or analyse it; that 'data handling' is yet another program, whose result is an 'anthropic program', a system of colours and lines on a screen that a trained human brain steeped in the appropriate context can 'execute' to construct a mental object, related in some way to the original data object, that is felt to "satisfy" the query.

Pushing the logic of our system back, we find that we encounter problems of this sort at every level: really, any time information has to pass from one part of the system or one stage of the algorithm to another. When there are multiple workers (grad students wielding whiteboard markers) in the room, the number of 'boundary problems' grows exponentially, depending on how the problem is 'orchestrated' (organised to be shared by many workers). Thinking about all data as a kind of program - potentially a very simple program - can be helpful: what ripples through the system is not really 'information', baldly understood, but patterns of behaviour that ultimately bring the global state in harmony with 'the problem'.

Now, imagine we take a simple approach to orchestration and say that every grad student has a particular role: perhaps one of them does all the differentiation and another does all the antidifferentiation, or perhaps one of them draws wonderfully neat tables and another exquisitely fine charts. If we want to put everyone to work simultaneously, one policy might be to assign a little whiteboard to each worker (perhaps one of those portable ones): that way they can all work without tripping each other up. But if one worker needs to act on the 'state' maintained by another worker, one could easily end up in a situation where an unusually fast worker erases something important before a slower worker can interpret it.

One solution here would be to allocate part of the available whiteboard space as a single shared 'commons', which workers may read from freely, but not write to freely. When a worker completes a task in their own local ('scratch') space, they submit their work to be integrated into the commons. If doing so would overprint something that another worker still needs, the integration can be delegated to the dependant worker to perform once their own work is complete; in any case, the author of the completed work is now free to take on some other task.

The overhead to manage this system is not as drastic as one might suspect at first. If every worker has a signature (perhaps the student's initials, assuming they're unique), we can imagine each worker taking a moment after accepting a task to carefully sign every part of the commons that they believe they will need to fulfil that task. A simple algorithm then ensures that the board will always be updated in due course. When a worker completes their task, they leave their working under the part of the whiteboard that needs to be replaced. Then they do a tour of the room and erase their signature from every part of the whiteboard where they had previously placed it. If theirs is the last signature to be erased from a particular part of the board, they check for any 'pending changes' (submitted whiteboards sitting on the ground) and transcribe them (we assume one needs not be expert in the subject matter of the submitted work to carry out the simple work of transcription). If we further ensure that only one worker is permitted to be up signing boards at any point in time, this algorithm guarantees that no-one will lose access to something they depend on while they are still working. (This sort of pattern could broadly be classified as a kind of 'semaphore' in the literature.)

# Tasks in space

We now have a way to manage the execution and submission of tasks: what we lack is any detail on how tasks are actually generated and distributed. Up to now, we have casually imagined that we (the instigator of the program) are standing in the room being consulted by idle grad students and handing them work to do based on the state of the boards at any given time. Even if we imagine replacing ourselves with a robot (some sort of ticket machine, maybe), this pattern creates a huge bottleneck - one can picture queues of grad students lining up for work. This bottleneck can be alleviated by providing fewer, bigger tasks, but that clashes directly with the task submission algorithm we just developed, which functions best when tasks are predictable in duration and ideally fairly small.

We can start to get a hold of this problem by performing a simple but subtle transformation: rather than viewing tasks as in some way separate from task outputs, we can instead imagine a 'task' as simply the natural evolution of a pattern of symbols on the boards themselves. Thus a worker can commence a task literally by applying themselves to any unallocated part of the board and simply taking the 'next most obvious step' in the given context. For example, if the 'context' is a physics classroom, a section of whiteboard that says "Ignoring air resistance, the time it takes for an apple weighing 100 grams to fall from a tree two metres tall in seconds is..." might invite a student to derive and write "0.64". One could then imagine 'second-degree' tasks where the desired 'evolution' is not a fact but another 'task': for example, the 'apple' challenge just given could itself have been generated by a student acting spontaneously on the prompt "The span of time wherein Isaac Newton has made a discovery but does not know it yet." Assuming we are endowed with a crop of competent, self-directed graduate students, it is easy to see how an execution model like this saves the problem-poser (i.e. ourselves) a great deal of time and effort.

The scenario become exponentially more complex if we allow the tasks to depend on each other. If there is no such interdependence - i.e. if our overall problem is 'embarrassingly parallel' - we might as well scatter the students into their own classrooms, each working on their own problem. We need barely trouble ourselves to organise such an effort; it is trivial. For better or for worse, most problems worthy of our attention demand more of us than that: a careful consideration of exactly what depends on what, and whether the costs of sharing the load exceed the encumbrance of managing a team.

Now that the 'tasks' are simply 'board states', like any other data, and given we already went to the trouble of defining a sign-in, sign-out system for managing the 'board state', our first instinct might be simply to permit each student to wander up to the board at their own pace, secure in the knowledge that all resources are preserved while in use. Unfortunately, such a model would result in non-deterministic behaviour, where - for instance - information that might have been important to a particular student's part of the endeavour comes and goes without that student ever seeing it. To preserve determinism - ensuring that whatever state the room achieves is the only state it could have achieved based on previous states in the given context - we need to give every student the opportunity to review every change that could possibly impact on their part of the job. That is no small challenge.

The 'task problem' is related to another problem we must overcome: one related to various assumptions we have made about how the 'whiteboards' themselves actually work.

It will be noted that we have thus far given no actual constraints on the dimensions and layout of this imaginary 'classroom' and its many whiteboards. The assumption is that the space is ample but finite, and that the whiteboards are arrayed in some arbitrary way that is not a priori optimised for our needs (whatever they may be). At the same time, we have casually spoken of 'parts of the board', of 'commons' and 'scratch' spaces, and various other constructs that imply a sound and stable division of the larger space into convenient subspaces.

Anyone who has worked with real groups of students in a real room with real whiteboards will know that none of these assumptions are entirely appropriate. Although we are still inside a thought experiment where we might plead 'irrelevant' to such charges, it will shortly be seen how the problems beleaguering our metaphor translate into 'real-world' challenges that we cannot dismiss so easily.

Let's imagine that the classroom has been initialised with a single overarching 'prompt' to be addressed; it doesn't matter what it is exactly, but we should imagine that it is succinct enough to be expressed using only a small amount of the available space. The first 'task' might be to break the problem into several sub-tasks, some of which will depend on each other. Tangibly, this will involve writing new prompts onto hitherto blank expanses of whiteboard, increasing the total proportion of space that is in use. Some of these prompts might be self-contained, while others might be partially or wholly reliant on facts derived from resolving other prompts; some might resolve to terminal 'facts', while others might evolve into one or more new prompts. What's common in all cases is that each new branch of the task graph involves changes in the allocation of space. If we assume that the space is palpably finite, we certainly do not have the luxury of leaving everything 'up on the board': we must erase as we go along. We have already described an algorithm (one of many viable choices) that helps us overcome one obvious problem that this occasions - the problem of knowing what can and can't be erased, and when - but that algorithm itself rested on a deeper assumption: that we have some way of addressing 'board space' in the first place. We may have the means to track who owns the *content* of the boards, but we never bothered to establish how the content itself is differentiated.

The obvious approach would be to draw boxes (potentially very complex boxes) around every chunk of 'signable' content. Each box permits a single 'master signature' - distinct from the 'delete me' signature list previously discussed - that denotes the single graduate student who is permitted to write or erase within that box; a box with no signature is free to be claimed by any idle worker, but a box with a prior signature is the sacred property of that worker and no-one else. Thus the authority to update or erase some swathe of the board can always be imputed to whoever is currently 'in charge' of the box that contains that swathe. The box could be established in full from the outset, or alternatively be allowed to change shape as needs evolve; it might even be fragmented across multiple different parts of the room, if contiguous space is not available. What is important is that the box is always sealed, so that there is never any confusion between 'owned' content and content which belongs something or someone else. 'Unsealing' the box's walls by erasing even one small part of it could be imagined to 'spill' the contents into the environment: at the top level, this would have the effect of making the contents "no-one's property" and implicitly releasing the occupied space to be used for other purposes.

This approach works well for 'flat' task graphs, but if we are to contemplate more complex problems, we must develop a more complex addressing system. The next step on this road would be to allow boxes inside boxes. For example, a worker tackling a particular problem might produce some interim figure early on which has consequences for other workers; if that worker takes care to circumscribe that potentially useful result, it can be preserved for others' benefit even when they themselves are done with it. Once we have the infrastructure to support one degree of nesting, we can support arbitrary nesting at only a modest cost to metadata complexity. As at the top level, erased boxes implicitly 'spill' their former contents into their environment, except that now the 'environment' might be another box, which gets to decide for itself whether the content is worth 'adopting' or not.

The final step would be to support 'multidependent' spaces: that is, boxes that overlap. This would be invaluable in cases where two problems ultimately imply a common sub-problem: instead of posing (and solving) it twice, the commonality could be recognised with a shared task that can be solved once for the benefit of both parties. The 'box' for this third task might reserve a swathe of whiteboard space for itself while simultaneously extending into the reserved spaces of its stakeholders, authorising it to work on behalf of both. When the shared task is resolved, its box is erased in the usual manner; any data now rendered 'homeless' is free to be erased and replaced by new tasks, while the 'lobes' of the shared task box lying within the domains of the stakeholders become the property of those stakeholders to do with as they will.

The new system allows us to improve on our earlier approach to 'signing' work in and out of the 'commons'. Now, instead of the 'commons' being a special, reserved part of whiteboard space, it is simply the content of the overarching enclosure: that is, the immediate content of the box drawn around the start prompt. 'Scratch' or 'local' spaces, for their part, are just boxes inside that box; if a worker wants to update a part of the board while someone else still depends on it, they can simply draw a box *inside* the task box and do their new work there. When the original is no longer required, its direct content can be erased and the child box 'promoted' by erasing its boundaries; a final nip-and-tuck of the outer boundary releases any unneeded space without affecting the identity of the object as a whole.

This system also clarifies and formalises our up-to-now fairly muddy notion of what a 'task' is and how one is allocated. Say a graduate student has applied themselves to a particular box and the prompt (initial state) it containts. Now say that same student reaches a point in their working where there are two or three sub-problems that all need solving; none of them depend on each other (at least, not that the student can tell), but the larger problem will eventually depend on all of them. The sub-problems, like everything else, are represented by ink on a whiteboard; but by the simple action of drawing a box around that ink, the problem description is elevated to a first-order task that could feasibly be undertaken by anyone. Since the parent job has ground to a halt, the student might assign one of these new sub-boxes to themselves; in due course, one of their fellow students may wander up to the same general vicinity, see the unassigned problem next door, and secure it for themselves. Thus the original student has delegated a task to a colleague without needing to exchange a single word (except perhaps for politeness). When all sub-problems are solved, that original student can erase the child boxes and release the contents back to the original box - which the student is still the proper 'owner' of - and proceed as if the sub-problems, and the unspoken collaboration that solved them, never existed.

We still need to address the matter of non-determinism in multi-worker scenarios: how do we ensure that critical information does not come and go at the pace of a 'fast worker' before a comparatively 'slow' worker has a chance to signal their interest? The new 'box' system offers something critical to this problem: it specifies a unique point in both space *and time* at which a particular piece of information becomes 'addressable'.

Imagine a particularly fast student applying themselves to the board in a flurry of genius. Perhaps they are taking a particularly thorny integral, and one particular interim product (some partial derivative, perhaps) would be a real timesaver for some of that student's peers. It would be totally unreasonable to expect that student to pause at every other line and say "Did you see that? Can I erase this? Do you still need that over there?" Indeed, this would be no less disruptive to the rest of the students - even those with a peculiar interest - because they would be constantly drawn away from their own work by the advertisements of their peer.

Instead, imagine each student observes a common policy to 'enclose' potentially valuable results as they go along. The overhead for the original author is minimal - how long does it take to draw a few straight lines? Yet this practice by itself almost completely resolves the non-determinism problem. The key is to invert the signing convention: now, instead of a worker signing objects that they require, workers sign the objects they do *not* require - as if to say, "My signature affirms that I won't complain if somebody erases this." The absence of such a signature implies the ongoing investment of that worker in a particular bundle of information. If there is a registry of all active workers somewhere, it is easy enough to identify when all potential stakeholders are accounted for and schedule that swathe of whiteboard for erasure - which itself can be made the responsibility, as before, of whoever makes the last amendment to the signature list. (If the population of workers is fixed, even the signatures can be omitted in favour of a single integer that decrements to zero at deletion time - again, like a classic semaphore.)

Our new algorithm will of course come unstuck if workers do not honour their obligation to sign whatever they do not need. Since the boxes are all uniquely identifiable, it would behoove the prudent student to keep a private list of known boxes and to compare this list with the whiteboard state at regular intervals; any new appearances that are obviously not relevant can be noted down in the list and signed on the spot. In a very large, complex classroom, one can imagine this labour growing into a real chore.

This is where the concept of 'nested boxes' comes into its own. Instead of requiring every worker to sign every box, one can instead institute a policy whereby a worker who 'opts out' of a particular box simultaneously implicitly opts out of every child box, *present or future*. This effectively empowers each workers to sign themselves out of entire branches of the task graph all at once. Imagining a tree with ten layers of ten children each on average (thus ten billion 'leaf boxes'), a worker with a focussed interest in less than a third of the boxes in the first layer, and in each of those, a third of the child boxes, and so on, may find themselves better off time-wise by a factor of a million or so - and this only at the modest cost of having to keep track of address strings that are ten tokens long, instead of merely one.

# Declaring success

We have gone into the weeds on how we expect our graduate students to actively tackle our posed problem. As the instigator of the entire exercise, we must not lose sight of what we ourselves ultimately hoped to gain from it, which is some answer (or answers) to our original question.

In real classroom exercises, it is customary to double-underline anything that is intended to be reviewed by the professor. We might imagine a similar protocol in action here, but it is complicated to some extent by our 'box' system, which was intended from the outset to remove our authority from parts of the board so that our workers could proceed without excessive supervision: adding a special type of 'board token' that cuts through that hierarchy would seem to defeat the point.

As is often so, the poison is also the remedy here, and we can envision an elegant solution to the problem of 'returning' answers by simply imagining that every box is the 'master' of the boxes therein. Thus our own mastery over the room as a whole is simply another parent-child relationship which should be expected to function exactly the same as all of the other, lower-level relationships. In true collaborative fashion, we could take this to its logical extreme and imagine that we ourselves are simply another graduate student (which has the virtue of being true, in the case of this author); we have our own signature, and just because the 'original box' (the imaginary 'box' surrounding the entire room in which we stand) is permanently imprinted with that signature does not suggest that we have superior status to anyone as such: we are delegating to our fellows now, and later, they may delegate to us.

In principle, a sub-problem that is truly 'resolved' should dissolve itself, releasing its content into the enclosing box. As the instigator of the whole exercise, the 'box' in which we imagine the final 'answer' materialising is simply the imaginary 'box' that is the classroom itself: that is, at some point in the future, we expect to be able to walk into this room and see nothing but whiteboards and emphatic 'final answers', with nary a 'box' to be seen. What this implies is that the aggregate 'answer' - the board state that satisfies us as the ultimate evolution of the initial state - must percolate up to that top level from wherever its various elements have been calculated, no matter how many layers down that might be.

Fortunately, the protocols we have already instituted carry out that duty automatically. If every student is diligent in signing off on boxes to be erased, then the contents of each box will naturally 'spill' into the parent space at the earliest possible time. The only matter we need to clarify is the distinction between a box that persists because it is being actively worked and a box that persists only because it is still being 'read' by someone (i.e. not everyone has signed it or one of its ancestors for erasure). The clearest way to do this is to gather together the various proclamations we have made and explicitly characerise the full, final 'lifecycle' of a box.

A box is born when the owner of another box draws a valid enclosure around some swathe of content in their own box. At birth, the box has no signatures on it at all: no 'delete me' signatures, and no 'master signature' either. The box's evolution begins when a student walks up to it and claims it by imprinting their own signature as the 'master' of that box. From this point on until that student deems the work complete, the content of the box is in flux.

While practically, of course, the content is visible to gawking bystanders, it would obviously be folly for any of the worker's peers to rely on that content while it is still being mutated: for all procedural purposes, it must be considered invisible, or indeed, totally nonexistent, until the editing is complete. On the other hand, if a box is declared inaccessible to all peers during editing, then the possibility of collaboration is totally foreclosed: by the time one's peers can see what's going on, the task is already complete. Thus we must make an exception: we permit a box's (completed) sub-boxes to be publicly visible, even if the parent box is not. There is a certain intuitiveness to this: effectively, we are saying each box must be allowed to have a 'life of its own', proceeding through the same stages as any other box, regardless of the state of the parent. (Imagine these as bright windows in the blackness of the parent, like a Sidney Nolan painting.)

With this ruling, the business of taking up a task appears in a new light. We can imagine a particular worker toiling 'in the dark' inside a particular box, there efforts invisible to their peers. That worker then draws up a potential sub-problem in a part of that board and, seeing the potential to delegate it, draws a box around it. From that moment, until the moment that someone comes along and secures the sub-box for themselves, the content of the sub-box is visible to all - standing effectively as an advertisement for itself ("Look at this fun problem! Come and solve me!"). Once a master signature is applied to a box, its content becomes invisible to the world once more, and the worker is free to work without disruption.

Eventually, the box may reach some 'terminal' state. Given that the worker signed in as 'master' is the only person who can legally 'see' the (free) content of their own box, it must be left entirely to their discretion to decide when this point has been reached. There is exactly one constraint we *must* place on the worker's judgement in this matter, which is that no box can be declared 'complete' until any and all child boxes are themselves 'complete'. This follows because, while the child boxes do not represent 'content' in themselves, they do represent the promise of content in the future, since - at some point - they may reach their own 'terminal state' and suddenly become visible in the context of the parent (and eventually even 'spill' that content into the body of the parent). If this were to happen *after* the parent had already been certified 'complete', it would be impossible to guarantee determinism: workers who believe they are acting on the same data might in fact be acting on different versions - one with the child content, one without.

When the worker is finally in a position to adjudge a box 'complete', it is beholded on them to announce that fact to the world. It doesn't really matter how precisely: perhaps they simply append a big tick next to their signature, or perhaps a gloating 'Q.E.D.' In any case, the box is now in a new state, where it can no longer be 'written' to, but it can be 'read' from. By default, all peers that have a presumptive stake in the box's parent must be presumed to have a stake in the box too: this is the fact organically captured by the initial emptiness of the 'delete me' list. Because the box's content (including the content of all children) is now finalised, peers have all the information they need to determine whether it is of interest or not.

Once all active peers have signed out of the box (or one of its ancestors), the box has reached the end of its lifecycle. The last peer to sign out does the polite thing and carefully erases the box's walls. From this point on, the box's content is indistinguishable from the rest of the parent's (free) content, and it is indeed just as if the child 'never existed'. The box's lifecycle is closed.

So far, so good. Yet our exposition of the box lifecycle actually reveals one more consideration which we have completely missed up to this point. Consider what it really means for a worker to be able to 'see' a part of the board remote from their own. The ability to do this only has value if what the worker 'sees' somehow impacts the way they go about their own work. If the impact is such that the worker's part of the board will subsequently evolve in a manner other than it would have, then the overall trajectory of the global solution becomes dependent on the relative rates at which different workers work - which brings us back to the problem of determinism.

To overcome this, we must make one final stipulation - one which reveals something fundamental about the whole exercise. If the system is to be non-deterministic, then each task - each 'box' - must be initialised with explicit dependencies; put another way, the scope of the board that is made available to inform the work of a particular student at a particular part of the board must be restricted. You could say that a worker who takes on a particular box is required to 'put the blinkers on', only casting their eyes over parts of the larger board state that are specifically relevant to their own work. Crucially, those parts of the board that a particular worker is permitted to 'peek' at during execution must be determined *ahead of time* - which further implies that a given box cannot be tackled until the things it depends on are explicitly known and explicitly finalised.

Since we already have a way to 'address' every part of the board at the box level, the means of specifying these dependencies are already in hand. That, however, is only a small part of the problem. The larger problem, to state it plainly, is that it is all so very *complex*; necessarily, irreducibly, and profoundly. It can be done - even as we have described it - but to do it well, and to do it efficiently - with a minimum of fuss and confusion - is quite simply very difficult.

# From boards to bytes

As in our Modelish thought experiment, we have striven to construct this second thought experiment in a purely tactile and physical idiom. It will hopefully not have escaped notice that the procedures outlined here are not purely speculative, but actually entirely practical, and genuinely can be used in a classroom or laboratory setting for team-based exploratory knowledge work.

Nevertheless, anyone with a basic grounding in applied computer science will recognise the parallels between the procedures described here and the data arrays and logic blocks of a conventional computer program. The whiteboards are like computer memory, variously divided between procedures (our 'tasks' or 'prompts'), datasets ('interim products'), and metadata (the students' signatures). The 'workers' - our graduate students - are like computational 'threads' or 'processes' (depending on the execution model), while we ourselves play the role of the 'entry' or 'master' process that instigates and governs the program as a whole.

The many problems we encountered 'in the classroom' will be recognisable to any programmer who has had to deal with complex information systems, particularly those involving 'concurrency' (where parts of the program are allowed to advance independently of each other). Many of the strategies we just laid out - for managing common resources, access, coordination, timing, race conditions, data integrity, and so on - would be considered quite advanced: the sorts of things that a trained software engineer would be expected to know. As with Eve's Modelish adventure from before, this exercise illustrates how a careful and deliberate framing of a given problem can bring even fairly 'advanced' techniques within reach of common sense.

While hopefully illuminating in itself, the true purpose of our classroom thought experiment was to establish the conceptual and intuitional infrastructure for an advanced technique for dissolving complex problems. We call it Imperative Analysis. With this technique, the missing pieces of Eve's Modelish paradigm will become apparent, and we will have more of what we need to transform our diagnostic and expository devices into genuinely useful machines for science.

# Imperative Analysis

In the world of programming, an 'imperative' is a command of some kind, and 'imperative programming' is a paradigm that envisions a program as a stateful object that changes in response to commands. Most command line interfaces (like Linux or Windows shells) operate in an imperative manner.

Imperative Analysis deploys intuitions familiar to imperative programming to make sense of programs and the things they seek to represent. It can be applied to arbitrary programs written in formal programming languages, but more broadly to any information system, as an aid for knowledge workers trying to understand a pre-existing system, design a new system, or think about systems generally.

Imperative Analysis is effectively a formalisation of the sorts of thought processes we just explored with our classroom experiment. It starts with the sketching out of a single giant 'whiteboard' for the system as a whole: a giant data object that can be thought of simply as the 'global state'. From there, the general idea is to identify all the agents in the system that are empowered to impact any part of that state in any way, and for each agent, where and what that impact is. At the end of the process, the analyst will have in their possession an abstract representation of the system that suggests not one, but many ways it could be implemented, depending on the resources available. As we saw with Eve in our Modelish thought experiment earlier, such alternate possibilities for how to build and operate a complex information system may often prove to be more effective than the original design; even when this is not so, the exercise may generate valuable insights into how the system works - or doesn't.

Firstly, let's a useful nomenclatural convention. An object's 'state' is the part of the sum of the information about an object that is subject to change, *viewed as a property of that object*. The information itself at any given time, viewed independently of that object, we shall call the 'configuration' or 'config' for short. When speaking about an object's state, we may be speaking abstractly or concretely, depending on the context (e.g. "object 'x' depends on the state of 'y'" versus "the state of object 'x' is 'six'"); but when it comes to the object's configuration, or equivalently the configuration of the state, it is always clear that we are speaking concretely. When an object's state changes, we say that the object has been 'reconfigured'. This simple lexical intervention allows us to avoid tempting but tortuous constructions like "the object's state has changed to a different state", and also empowers us to speak more directly about how information moves from one object to another: it is not the 'state' that moves (that is nonsensical, since a state is by definition 'of something'), but rather the configuration that moves (so an object "adopts the configuration" of another object, say). The distinction between a state and its configuration is like the difference between a variable and its value, or an array and its data: it is the difference between saying "the 'five' changes into a 'six'" (sensible but strictly nonsensical), and the more correct "the value changes from five to six". It is always important to preserve this distinction, particularly when we are speaking from an internal perspective where the actual referent (the thing that the information is 'about') is unclear or difficult to articulate, and we are unable to access phrasing like "the object's state" and are forced to speak of the state by itself.

Now, the method of Imperative Analysis starts with an overarching agent we dub the Imperator. The Imperator plays the role that we ourselves played in the classroom thought experiment: the agent in the system who can see everything and alter anything.

The 'classroom' in this formalisation is the domain under the power of the Imperator - the global 'state', imagined as a literal 'state', like Belgium. For the sake of analysis, we imagine the state to be infinitely capacious (unlike Belgium), but not inherently structured in any way: an endless vista of green fields, blank whiteboards, and unlimited Turing tape.

Now, as in our classroom experiment, it is given that the global state - is set to some initial configuration by the Imperator (like the 'original prompt' we imagined), and that there is some final configuration that is recognisable by the Imperator as the intended product or products of the system (like the underlined 'final' answers we expected of our students). In between, there are configurations that are in some sense 'provisional' or 'contingent'. At any given time, the global state responds to a generic unary action of 'being worked' by reconfiguring itself in some specific way. After $n$ discrete repetitions of that action - call them 'epochs' - the state reaches a configuration where it is invariant under work - that is, further iterations do not prompt it to change at all. (We will presume an acyclic state graph for now; the case of loops will be discussed later.) Crucially, the Imperator (the 'global worker', if you will) does not have any internal memory or the power of discernment to choose what future configuration will be achieved: the complete trajectory of the state is a foregone conclusion from the moment it is initialised. (If it helps, imagine the Imperator as a 'good emperor' who faithfully implements an unchanging constitution over a changing polity.)

Up to this point, we have effectively described a Turing Machine - specifically, a 'universal Turing machine' where the machine state, the head position, and the 'program' itself are all written on the tape. Such a framing literally describes all conceivable conventional computations - albeit at the cost of not saying very much of interest. Take a simple Python program - for now, we will assume it is very simple, with no loops, no recursion, and no inputs (i.e. no disk reads, user input, or web queries). Such a program could be represented completely in this framing simply by taking the full memory footprint of the program as the 'Imperium' and taking each call to the global interpreter as a new epoch. At this very high level of analysis, the sole 'imperative' being issued by the Imperator is a world-weary "Next!"

Now, say we take our standard Python program and analyse it line by line - like Eve did with her laboratory code. Instead of reading it semantically ("here is 'x', 'x' goes into 'f'..."), we read it imperatively, specifically scanning for every point where a change to computer memory is implied. In Python, the memory state changes any time a statement is fired and any time an expression is evaluated. Say we take some 'magic words' - `foo`, `bah`, `qux`, `zap`, `vim`, `lop`, and so on - and assign one to each such 'inflection point' in the code. The program then ceases to be a single state that mutates through the epochs, but resembles something more like an incantation - `foo, qux, zap, bah, lop, vim ...` - where each utterance is an imperative that commands the global state to reconfigure itself in a certain way based on the logic of the imperative and the configuration of the present. It is what the Imperator must command to cause the global state to evolve in the proper way.

Though for a first pass we might assume that every imperative is uttered once, if we look more closely at the implied logic of each imperative, we are likely to find that some are effectively the same. Thus two imperatives, `foo` and `bah`, that were originally characterised as separate, can both be assigned to `foo` instead. To make this determination, we need to be able to guarantee that for every valid initial state, for a given incantation (sequence of imperatives) invoked thereon, for every invocation of `foo` in that incantation, we could instead have invoked `bah` to the same effect. This is a weaker constraint - in fact, a much weaker constraint - than the definition of equivalence for mathematical functions, where for functions $f$ and $g$ over some $x$ we would usually have to guarantee $f(x) = g(x)$. Here, `foo` and `bah` could be not only implemented differently, but actually be capable of affecting the state in very different ways, if given the opportunity; if however we can guarantee that they will *never* be given an opportunity to vary (because the circumstances in which they are invoked are always such that these differences are not manifest), we can still treat them as the same imperative within the scope of this analysis. If we repeat this step several times, we may find a good many effective duplicates and reduce the required inventory of imperatives dramatically.

Now, in any programming language - Python included - it is very rarely the case that a single inflection point in the code causes a complete reconfiguration of the state. It is equally rarely the case that a single inflection point depends on the entire global state: most of the time, the bit that changes will change in the same way regardless of the configuration of some irrelevant bit on the other side of the program. This is the next step of Imperative Analysis: tracking the read-scope and write-scope of every imperative. The read-scope comprises the whole of the global state which, being any different, would change the result of the imperative; the write-scope is that part of the global state and only that part that will change when the imperative is executed. It can help to visualise these scopes as 'territories' of the 'country' that is the global state: blue-bordered for the read-scope, red-bordered for the write-scope. It is not necessarily the case that these overlap in full or at all, nor indeed is it necessary for either scope to be greater than zero in extent: it could be that the imperative reads nothing and writes blindly, or it could be that the imperative reads everything and writes nothing (perhaps because it is doing something useful beyond the scope of the program, like a `print` call). It is also not actually necessary that these scopes be contiguous, though for the sake of comprehension, we're going to picture them that way for now. (The whole idea of 'contiguousness' in the context of state analysis is an artifact of the visual metaphor anyway - albeit one that will later suggest some powerful architectural strategies.)

As soon as the scopes of each imperative are drawn, two things become immediately apparent.

Firstly, there will likely be imperatives whose scopes are completely disjoint (not intersecting at all). From the perspective of these imperatives, they might as well be executing in totally different programs: nothing that one does has a bearing on the other and vice versa. This allows us to revisit the chain of imperatives (the 'incantation') and break it up into a graph, potentially with bifurcating paths where multiple imperatives could in principle be invoked simultaneously without impacting each other, because the parts of the state they depend on and affect do not overlap. If there are chains of such parallel imperatives to be invoked, it may strain the imagination (and the CPU) to have the Imperator multitask across every branch at once. At this point, we would be better to imagine the imperatives as being presided over by some delegated authority: a 'magistrate', let's say. Each magistrate is endowed with a certain 'magisterium' over which to hold sway and permitted to interpret and implement the common purpose over the imperatives and swathes of state they have been entrusted with. Thus Imperative Analysis begins to reproduce the collaborative classroom environment of the whiteboard experiment - and not coincidentally.

The second thing that becomes apparent after scoping is that there are now continuous swathes of the global state that are not traversed by any scope boundary. These are the true 'state variables' of the system, allowing the single global state to be divided into a constellation of 'statelets', each a realm unto itself. In this manner, a global state that is conceptually continuous (or typically so vast in terms of logical bits that it might as well be) can be discretised into a (potentially large but) finite set of independent units that can be named, tracked, and stored in isolation. This allows the read- and write-scopes of each imperative to be recast as simple discrete sets: the read-scope is the set of statelets that the imperative needs, and the write-scope is the set of statelets that the imperative alters. Once this is achieved, the actual underyling structure of the global state becomes irrelevant, and degrees of freedom for greater memory efficiency are opened up everywhere. (For example, if there is a data array somewhere in the program where one column is only ever accessed by one part of the program and the other column is only ever accessed in another, it might occur to us now to break up that array into two pieces and store each piece closer to the place where it is actually needed.)

At this point, the original program has already been altered beyond recognition: if analysis is literally the art of 'breaking down', we have succeeded in breaking the program into the largest possible discrete pieces - and most likely, a great number of them. If at this point we seize the opportunity to rearrange those pieces in space and time, we will almost certainly discover an alternative architecture that achieves the same ends as the original program but in a much more efficient and expressive way. That is a win in itself. However, we cannot lose sight of the fact that we deliberately chose a very simple program for this analysis - without common and powerful features like loops or recursion - and that we also still rely on the Imperator to sound off the imperatives one by one, more like a bored wizard than an emperor.

To move forward from here, we have to flip the analysis on its head and view the execution of the program not from the perspective of the actors, but from the perspective of the acted-upon - that is, from the perspective of the statelets.

Consider the statelet `a`. What is this program from the perspective of `a`? At `t=0`, the statelet is rewritten with the desired initial configuration. After that, many epochs may pass where nothing particularly happens. (We might say that the 'local epoch' does not increment during this time.) At some point, a magistrate comes along and issues an imperative commanding the statelet to change in a certain way. This might happen many times. When the program eventually halts (because one of the 'success' or 'failure' configurations has been reached), each statelet could produce a recorded history of every imperative it was personally subject to through the whole run of the program.

Imagine this record is available at runtime. It becomes no longer necessary to explicitly require imperative `bah` to be issued after imperative `foo`. Instead, the imperative `bah` simply comes with a logical condition: "I can only be invoked when the statelets I depend on report that they have received the action of `foo`." Call this the 'imperative predicate' in that it is the thing about the imperative's dependencies that must be true if the imperative is to take effect. Instead of the Imperator invoking one imperative at a time 'from the top', each imperative can be assigned its own dedicated magistrate who simply checks the revelant statelets every epoch - or better, 'subscribes' to those statelets and naps until they signal a change - and invokes the given imperative as soon and whenever the predicate rings true. The magistrate 'signs' the history of the statelet on behalf of the executed imperative, thereby triggering any imperatives watching that statelet, and the program goes on.

The virtue of this approach is that it entirely separates control from content. Imperatives are identified by simple strings (`foo`, `bah`, etc.), as are the statelets (`x`, `y`, etc.). Each statelet's history is just a sequence of strings - the names of the imperatives that affected it - and each imperative's predicate is just a logical sentence which queries the histories of the statelets in its read-scope and produces a verdict - go or no go. The predicates themselves could be arbitrarily complicated. One could use first-order logic or formal grammar - anything that acts on strings will do. An imperative `foo` dependant on statelet `x` could have a predicate $P$ that effectively searches the whole history of `x`, implementing logic like: "I will execute if the first signature is `bah` and the last signature is `qux`, unless the second-last signature is `zap`, in which case I won't execute unless the total number of signatures is greater than seven." A program equipped with imperative predicates of this kind and of this complexity could easily support massively intricate control flows that would be almost impossible to implement in a conventional language like Python.

This is big step forward for the framework - but it brings with it serious complications that must be considered and accommodated carefully.

The first obvious challenge is the problem of 'simultaneous write': if two imperatives trigger at the same time, and both write (wholly or partly) to the same statelet, those changes may clash, and what's more, the history becomes muddled (which imperative 'actually happened' - `foo` or `bah`?) The most direct way to tackle this is to reserve the business of actually mutating the state to the Imperator, who sees and knows all. Instead of changing the state themselves, each imperative instead submits the proposed changes to the Imperator as a sort of 'bill of works' - specifically, one bill for each affected statelet. The Imperator considers each bill on its merits. If multiple bills are pending for a given statelet in a given epoch, the Imperator carefully awaits all of them. Only when all proposed changes have been submitted does the Imperator actually put them into force, mutating the state on behalf of the executed imperatives. Crucially, it either finds a way to do so that validates all imperatives - allowing conflicting imperatives to 'co-sign' the statelet as an equal collective - or it crashes the whole program (potentially delegating the responsibility to reconcile the database to some higher authority, if any exists). That way, an imperative - once executed - can be certain that its signature will appear on the statelet eventually, if the program continues at all.

This new 'delayed write' protocol has the important side-effect of changing the nature of the histories upon which predicates are expected to act. Now, instead of being a sequence of strings (the names of the imperatives that affected it, in order), each statelet hosts a sequence of sets of strings - one set for each epoch, containing the names of all of the imperatives that affected it in that epoch. Set-based predicates will have no trouble dealing with a structure of this kind, but conventional grammar-based predicates will fail unless there is a pre-processing layer that flattens the structure into a string before the predicate is applied - not difficult to do, but important to do carefully.

The second obvious challenge in the new system arises when we consider the renewed possibility of looping, We deliberately excluded this from consideration before, but the new predicate system implicitly permits it: there is nothing stopping `foo` from triggering whenever `bah` triggers, or even when it itself triggers. This is a useful and desirable trait by itself, but it is useless if there is no way to subsequently close a loop.

Strictly speaking, there is *one way* to close such a loop using the infrastructure we already have: we could envisage a predicate that returns `True` if the last `n` actions on `x` are `foo` but `False` in the case of `n+1` actions. Effectively, we can support finite, deterministic loops, but not conventional conditional loops - equivalent to a language that provides `for` loops but not `while` loops. Actually, such languages do exist - Epigram is an example - and they are preferred in some cases because they avoid the 'halting problem': Turing's classic finding that it is not always possible to prove that a given program will eventually 'halt' (i.e. resolve either successfully or unsuccessfully). The halting problem is fatal for any language that operates in an environment with strictly limited resources, or which is absolutely required to deliver a binary outcome in finite time - for example, automated theory provers, financial transaction resolvers, or heavy database engines. Regular expressions (and related pattern-matching languages) are a familiar example of a deliberately Turing-incomplete language which most people rely on in one context or other every day. For these sorts of applications, the framework we have outlined thus far is already sufficient. If, however, we would like to apply Imperative Analysis to a general programming language like Python - which is non-negotiable if the purpose is to be useful to scientists - we need to augment the framework to permit recursion and conditional loops, with all the many consequences that ensue.

Our inability to close loops ultimately flows from our lack of support for branching behaviour. At present, when `foo` acts on `x`, `x` may be reconfigured in all sorts of ways, but the signature on `x` will always ultimately be `foo`. Since every signature is both a sign that "`foo` was here" as well as effectively a prompt for other imperatives to act, the outcome is in effect 'excessively predetermined'. Without branching, there is no way for the program to cease looping in a conditional manner (i.e. in a manner dependant on the actual information in the state) because the decision to exit a loop is itself a kind of branch: in one world, the loop continues, and in another world, it doesn't.

This appraisal of the problem suggests an obvious, but (as we've discussed) deeply consequential solution: we can choose to decouple imperatives from their signatures and allow any given imperative to sign a statelet in different ways depending on the internal logic of the imperative. For example, if imperative `foo` reads statelet `x` - a boolean, let's say - and finds that it is `True` in some way, it might reconfigure statelet `y` in whatever manner and sign it with the string `abra`; if on the other hand the value was deemed to be `False`, it might reconfigure `y` differently and subsequently sign it `cadabra` instead. Other imperatives depending on `y` (that is, with `y` in their read-scope) may have predicates that care very much whether the signature is `abra` or `cadabra` and may be invocable in one case and non-invocable in another. Thus a very small channel is created for the actual configuration of the global state to 'bleed information' up into the orchestration layer and affect the unfolding of the program.

Now that imperatives are 'signing' statelets with arbitrary strings instead of their own name (or at least, are not restricted as such), the characterisation of these strings as 'signatures' feels out of date. Whereas before, an imperative was simply signalling "I was here", they are now signalling something more like "Such-and-such is true of this thing". It would be more appropriate to call this kind of sign a 'certification', and the entity that administers it the 'certifier'. For example, say `foo` acts on `x`; perhaps it sets all of its values to null. The `foo` imperative (or rather the magistrate acting on its behalf) submits the bill of work to the Imperator and, once the change is made, applies the certification `is_blank`. Another imperative, `bah`, is predicated to act only on `x` when `x` is certified `is_blank`; it doesn't know that the certifier was `foo`, and it doesn't need to know, in order to have confidence that the statelet is appropriately configured for its needs. Reinterpreting signatures as certifications also gives more meaning to the phenomenon of multiple signing, where - in a given epoch - several different imperatives leave a mark on a given statelet. If these marks are interpreted as certifications (claims that a certain thing is 'true' of it), it doesn't seem incongruous at all that multiple such marks may have been left, since multiple conditions can perfectly well be true of the same thing. We will keep the terminology of 'signature' for the special sub-case where there is exactly one mark of certification and it happens to be the imperative's own name; that is of no significance to the execution of the program, but may be of semantic significance to us as its interpreters.

With branching comes looping, recursion, and all the panoply of consequences that freight a Turing-complete language. The level of complexity one chooses to embrace within this framework entirely depends on the task at hand and the appetite of the user. If one carefully designs imperative predicates that never fire simultaneously, one can avoid set-like histories and treat every statelet's history as a simple, flat string of signatures. If one enforces a rule that every imperative must sign the statelet with its own name, branching and looping are ruled out, but the rest of the framework persists. If one requires every predicate to act only on the most recent signature of each dependency, the complex control flows are ruled out and the program will proceed cleanly and predictably. If one endows every imperative with scopes that encompasses the complete global state, we go back to a very simple chain of global state changes, one after another. The correct approach in any case is the one that eschews as much complexity as possible. The purpose of Imperative Analysis is to produce representations of the system under study and make it obvious where there is spurious complexity and where there is irreducible complexity. Just because a given script is written in Python - a Turing-complete general-purpose programming language - doesn't mean that the 'imperativised' version of that program needs all those features: indeed, many if not most scientific workflows 'in the wild' are a great deal simpler than they appear to be. Every `while` loop is a `for` loop waiting to be tamed.

Before we close this introduction to Imperative Analysis, there are two more features we should introduce: features that tie this discussion closely to the other aspects of our general modelling framework.

Imperative Analysis is, as the name suggests, an 'analytical' technique. Its function is to break down its subject into the 'chunkiest' independent pieces possible; invariably, these pieces end up being smaller than we humans would like, and often too small to clearly suggest any structural or semantic symmetries that may be present, which if identified, could aid in the reconstruction of the program in a more powerful, efficient, and expressive way. Analysis is the enemy of abstraction, for better or for worse.

To achieve the best of both worlds, analysis should be followed by synthesis: the pieces, once shattered, should be glued together, better and stronger. Doing this without compromising the clarity and austerity of the newly analysed program is the hard part.

The most obvious place to pursue some useful resynthesis is in the topology of the global state. We never really specified what the 'state' was, and for good reason: as we eventually demonstrated, the state is ultimately broken up into discrete chunks as a consequence of the inherent discreteness of the program that ran over it. These chunks - the 'statelets' - are for all intents and purposes single variables, existing in an unordered set, addressable by name: the ultimate post-analysis entity, ripe for restructuring.

We now introduce 'statoids': effectively 'virtual statelets'. Every statoid is an entity, addressable by name, whose content is a set of statelets or other statoids. We shall declare that the content is addressable by name using dot notation: for example, `data.subdata` accesses the substate `subdata` from the statoid `data`. Viewed as a tree, the statelets form the 'leaves', and the statoids serve as intermediate nodes between the statelets and the global state, which can now be reconstrued as a kind of 'root statoid'.

Statoids immediately demonstrate their usefulness by cutting down on the number of free names in circulation. Thus far, it has been given that statelets can be directly referenced by name, without specifying how exactly this is facilitated (implicitly, like everything else, we assumed the Imperator would see to it). If the states are instead viewed as nodes of a tree, then we can assume that names are simply looked up in the tree, starting with the 'root statoid' (the 'global state proper', as it were), and working down. Thus if `a` is a statoid connected to the root node, `a.b` would implicitly be a node `b` that is included in the statoid `a` (if it exists). In a program with thousands or millions of statelets, hierarchicalising the state tree in this way is indispensable, both for ease of lookup and for basic semantic sense-making. If we still want `b` to be accessible by name from the top in addition to being accessible via `a`, there is nothing stopping us from connecting the node representing the `b` to be connected both to the `a` node and the root node. Indeed, there is no reason why it has to be known by the same name when accessed via either route, since a statelet's name was only ever a means of accessing it from the perspective of the root: we could make the 'name' a property of the connection, not of the node, and have the statelet's connection to the root be under the name `c` (allowing it to be referenced directly as `c`) and its connection via the statoid `a` to be under the name `b`. (Coincidentally, this is how Python's namespaces work - one of the original features for which it is most celebrated and emulated, and which is considered by its own authors to be "a honking great idea".)

Facilitating access is a good reason to introduce the statoid in itself - but we can do much more. The main service the statelets provided to the larger system was to store a record of the various imperatives that acted on it epoch after epoch. Statoids can provide the same service, but abstracted over its constituent substates. In any epoch, let the set of certifications on the statoid be the intersection of the certification sets of all substates. The intersection is easily taken regardless of whether the substates are statelets or are themselves statoids, since if they are statoids, their certification set is already the union of their children's sets, and so on recursively. With statoids in the mix, many predicates become much simpler to write. For example, if an imperative `foo` depends on statelets `x`, `y`, and `z`, and expects all three of them to be certified `abra`, then instead of checking `abra` on `x`, then `y`, then `z`, it could instead check them all at once on a suitable statoid encompassing all three.

If statoids can be used to abstract the 'read' action, can we also use them with the 'write' action? Certainly we can. Remember that there was nothing particularly special about how the statelets were drawn up in the first place: they simply represented the largest discrete chunks that could be treated as persistent entities, based on the implicit expectations of the different 'inflection points' in the code. Any imperative that knows how to deal with statelets `x`, `y`, and `z` in isolation necessarily also knows how to deal with a statoid over the same: that is after all how they appeared to begin with. Of course, statoids do not actually carry their own mutable 'history' - they are pure abstractions over the statelets, which are the sole stores of state in the system. Instead, write actions on a statoid can be submitted to the Imperator as bills of work on the constituents, and the final certification can be applied to each constituent individually. When the statoid's certification set is read back in the next epoch, it will show the applied certification just as if it were a statelet. While it is important that the Imperator knows the real structure of the state, there is no reason why any particular imperator needs to be aware of this unless they choose to be. If statoids can be accessed, read, and written to exactly as if they were statelets, we have achieved the fullest abstraction possible.

We have usefully resynthesised the global state: can we now do something similar for the logical layer - that is, the imperatives? At present, the 'analysed' forms of the imperatives are both incredibly insular and excessively cosmopolitan. In a typical program, a single imperative might be responsible for toggling the state of a single boolean in response to some change in another boolean; there could easily be millions of these in any sizeable program. Simultaneously, each imperative is expected to supervise the entire global state, ever poised to spring into action as soon as its predicate is satisfied. (This is exactly the reason why most programming languages are strictly procedural: it is much easier to let each imperative fire in an arbitrary sequence under the presumption of total prior dependency than it is to carefully tease out the actual degrees of freedom in the system; the net cost of such wasteful architectures can scarcely be calculated.)

The improvements we just made to state management have to some extent enhanced imperative flow already. Consider an imperative `foo` that reacts to the certification of three different statelets as `abra`. If these statelets are bundled into a statoid, `foo` can subscribe solely to that statoid and be spared the overhead of listening to all three separately. Similarly, when writing state, an imperative can now address several statelets at once: it needn't be aware of the 'actual' structure of the state.

If we want to enhance imperative management further, we need to do the same sort of thing to imperatives that we just did to the state: hierarchicalise them. We can introduce a new kind of entity: the 'routine', which is a structure that can be invoked just like an imperative, but which takes effect by delegating that invocation to the component 'subroutines' - which may be either imperatives or other routines. As with statoids, there is no particular reason why imperatives should be allocated exclusively to particular routines, and one could imagine a graph structure where certain imperatives, or even routines, are included in multiple different routines, giving alternate pathways to 'trigger' the same underlying imperative (under the proviso, of course, that each imperative is ultimately only fired once per epoch at most). Routines, like statoids, allow us to provide potentially crucial clues to the Imperator about how we expect the program to run and what we expect it will need, without disrupting or altering the fundamental underlying logic of the analysis in any way.

To make statoids work, we needed to decide on a method for merging the important features of the children, which we did by taking the intersection of the certification set. The active feature of imperatives is their predicates. If the invoking of a routine is supposed to invoke all its components, then it follows that the 'implicit predicate' of the routine must be in some sense the 'intersection' of the component predicates: that is, it must be a predicate that rings true if and only if all component predicates ring true. This is trivial to do in any predicate language that includes a logical conjunction operator or something equivalent, but that is hardly much of an improvement over the alternative, since the conjunction of $p$ and $q$ still requires the evaluation of both components, just as it would if we never bothered to conjoin them at all. If the predicate evaluation engine is a little smarter than your average bear, there may be opportunities to 'memoise' (quickly cache and retrieve) the results of certain logical nodes that may happen to be shared between predicates; this  and similar computing tricks could very easily accelerate evaluation dramatically, in the right circumstances. But if such techniques are available to us, we might as well apply them globally, and not bother with the overhead of 'routines' at all.

To make the abstraction of routines more meaningful, we need to not only conjoin the component predicates but take the further step of doing some reduction on that conjunction. For example, say we are using a simple predicate language with common English constructions like 'and', 'or', and 'if'. If we have two predicates, "$p$ is true if $r$ is true" and "$q$ is true if $r$ is true and $s$ is not true", the conjunction produces an obvious redundancy: "$l$ is true if $p$ and $r$ are both true, where $p$ is true if $r$ is true and $q$ is true if $r$ is true and $s$ is not true". It would be much simpler to write "$l$ is true if $r$ is true and $s$ is not true$. This saves us multiple evaluations - a big time-saving.

For more complex predicates written in more sophisticated predicate languages, simplification may produce control flows that are dramatically faster to evaluate - and probably much easier to comprehend too. Nothing comes for free, of course, and the cost of this strategy is two-fold. Firstly, it could dent the effectiveness of other acceleration tactics (like memoisation) which might prefer a more atomic representation. Secondly, it requires us to do some additional maths that cannot safely be assumed to be trivial. To address both concerns, we need to embrace, not shun, the additional complexity we have added.

On the second point, note that the work of simplifying predicates only needs to be done once - when the program is 'compiled' (that is, 'locked in' to its final form for execution). Conceivably, this might be done just once, at the time the program is written, and never again for the rest of human history; for a program that is not merely facile, such a measure is worth taking at almost any cost. On the first point - and in light of what we have just noted about compilation - consider that we do not have to tackle each predicate in isolation. If we know what the routinisation strategy is going to be for the whole program - as we certainly do know, well ahead of run-time, given that we ourselves wrote the program - then we can 'crunch the numbers' across all of them simultaneously.

If we continue with down this developmental pathway, we end up with a sort of 'predicate graph' where the concerns of acceleration and simplification are balanced. For example, if two predicates could be merged under a routine by eliminating some sub-expression $p$, but $p$ happens to crop up in other places anyway, we might choose *not* to apply that simplification, because in any given epoch, the trivially conjoined predicate will have the speed advantage of being able to borrow the already-evaluated $p$ from somewhere else. Better still, the graph can be analysed for entirely new terms that never actually appeared in any of the predicates, pre- or post-simplification: logical facts that can be seen to be true in view of the whole graph that weren't obvious from the perspective of particular imperatives or routines. These 'implied predicates' could say things like "`foo` always happens after `bah`", offering shortcuts that skip whole swathes of the graph in a given epoch.

The decision to abstract up to a predicate graph ties in with routinisation in two ways. Firstly, note that a routine is more than just an object of convenience: it also communicates to the implementation a certain intuition about which things are likely to happen together most frequently. Graphs come with both costs and benefits, and optimising a pathway that is never used at the expense - even trivial - of a critical pathway is never wise. Secondly, there has to be some limit to any analysis: if we analyse everything down to fine dust, the whole structure falls apart. The institution of a routine projects a certain confidence about the structure of the graph from that point down - not a true 'encapsulation', necessarily, but a signal to 'approach with caution'. Thirdly, 'implied' predicates may themselves suggest good targets for routinisation, which may not have been obvious before. If it is implied that `foo`, `bah`, and `qux` will almost always fire together, and it is apparent on inspection that this is likely to happen very frequently, one should strongly consider bundling them as a routine. This will strengthen not only the structure of the program, but its semantics: after all, Imperative Analysis is meant to be a tool for *understanding* systems, as much as for building and running them.

There are many tradeoffs to be considered in all this, of course, and we are only ever really guessing (or rather, modelling) what will ultimately prove to be more efficient, given that - after all - we don't really know how the program will unfold in reality (if we did, we wouldn't need to run it in the first place). What's important is that the work only needs to be done once and that it only impacts the implementation and not the underlying logic. As modellers in a shared ecosystem, it is good enough a lot of the time to simply assume that the engine we are using knows how to optimise these sorts of things. One of the great benefits of breaking up a workflow to such a high degree is that the number of 'attachment points' for common ingenuity are greatly multiplied. (And as we have noted elsewhere, compiler techniques in particular have come an awfully long way since the days of C and Fortran.)

If we are comfortable assuming that predicates can be combined in a gainful way, our newly introduced 'routines' are likely to deliver substantial advantages in simplicity and performance. The other end of execution - the write stage - may enjoy similar advantages. Since the firing of a routine implies the firing of all subroutines, we can be certain of receiving a 'bill of works' from each. (Given that routines do not exercise an exclusive ownership over their constituent imperatives, we must be wary of receiving and accidentally implementing the same bill twice - but this is quite manageable with a simple checklist.) We haven't specified much about how these 'bills' are actually reconciled by the Imperator, as we have deliberately avoided describing the architecture of the 'global state' generally. However, as with the evaluation of predicates, we should recognise that there is a world of difference between something being logically sound and being computationally manageable. Whether the underlying 'buffer' supporting our state is a computer memory core or a classroom full of whiteboards, efficiency in reading and writing is a first-order concern in any program actually destined for execution. Though the 'how' needn't (and shouldn't) contaminate the 'what' unduly, we need to ensure that the way our program is laid out will eventually support the right optimisations in the right places, whatever they may be.

Even without knowing anything in particular about how writing works, there are a few ways the Imperator can take advantage of the bundling of write-actions by routines. The very existence of a routine implies that the program anticipates it will be invoked from time to time. That tacitly guarantees that, at some point, the statelets within the collective write-scope of the routine will all have to be edited at the same time. If so, it might make sense to store those statelets together, so that they can be conveniently accessed as a unit. (This is especially neat if those statelets also happened to be organised as a statoid.)

The decision may be complicated if there are compelling alternate considerations - perhaps some of the constituent imperatives are expected to fire more often independently than apart, or perhaps there is a competing routine that expects some of the same statelets to be bundled in a different way. As with the predicate synthesis we proposed, these are all 'swings and roundabout' of the implementation itself, with no real bearing on the ultimate logical implications (and thus ultimate evolutionary trajectory) of the system, but potentially signficant consequences for the efficiency of execution. It only falls upon us to suggest what seems intuitive, and to trust the Imperator to square every circle - even if the solution is a little rough. In the case of competing routines, for example, the Imperator might choose to interleave the underlying data buffers so that both sets of statelets are accessible as groups, albeit slightly less conveniently than if we only had to optimise for one of them. Alternatively, it might be able to prepare in advance a fast algorithm for restructuring computer memory at runtime, so that it appears structured one way for the convenience of one routine and another way for the convenience of the other - at the cost of managing the 'track switch' each time. Such compromises are part and parcel for smart implementations.

Are there any deeper benefits to structuring our imperatives into routines? If we imagine there is some sort of 'memory writing protocol' that is the write-stage kin of the predicate logic we described earlier, it may occur to us to ask whether write actions can be 'conjoined' in a similar way to predicates. For example, if two students are both working in the same classroom and one is asked to erase the whiteboard on the left and the one in the centre while the other is asked to erase the one in the centre and the one on the right, there is an obvious opportunity to save some trouble by refraining from erasing the centre board twice.

Any memory read-write protocol worth its salt will have a repertoire of such tricks up its sleeve, and again, the key is to ensure our program architecture exposes the largest surface possible to allow the magic to happen. This poses a problem for us, because until now, we have been happy to leave the write stage as a kind of 'black box', assuming imperatives have unlimited authority to mutate the state as they wish (more precisely, to demand that the Imperator does so on their behalf). Such an unrestrained prerogative is what was behind the idea of the 'write scope' in the first place: it is exactly why we went to all the trouble of discretising the 'statelets' as we did. Though we could throw our hands up here and be content that the Imperator will at least know well enough to make any obvious optimisations *at runtime* (being able to signal changes to several statelets at once is certainly helpful), this is a far cry from the efficiencies we successfully pursued with our compile-time approach to predicates.

Though we are bound by our own logic to concede that a given imperative *may* alter any and all parts of its write-scope, that is not necessarily to say that it *will*. Imagine a given imperative, `foo`, views its write-scope as a switchboard, which it shares with another imperative, `bah`. Imperative `foo` may be required to toggle switch $A$ if a particular indicator light is green, switch $B$ if it is red, and in any case, always toggle switch $C$. If `bah` only needs to toggle switch $C$ some of the time, and the two coexist within a single 'work unit' (a routine), then in a sense, then the logic of `bah` regarding $C$ is redundant. Similarly, if imperative `bah` is directed to always flick switch $A$ to 'off' (regardless of its prior state), then the logic of `foo` regarding $A$ is redundant too.

We have to be very careful how we proceed here, because - at the end of the day - Imperative Analysis is an analytical technique, not a programming language. We cannot and should not specify exactly how imperatives do what they do, or why: after all, depending on what we're analysing, an 'imperative' could correspond to a binary operator, a multiple-terabyte data aggregation, or even a policymaker enacting some change to law.

What we *can* do is presuppose the existence of some sort of shared mutation protocol across any given set of imperatives that are acting in the same domain, and stipulate what such a protocol will need (and what it will need to *do*). This is no different from the assumption we made about predicates - that they are all written in the same language, though we never said exactly what. As with our predicates, we know that the write protocol needs to pivot on runtime behaviour while still offering something useful that allows compile-time optimisations to be pursued. The main difference is that, whereas the predicates ultimately relied on already 'public' information about which statelets had been certified in what ways, the write protocol must pivot on some other sources of information which have hitherto been 'private' to the imperatives.

The idea of a 'promise to write' in a certain way is precisely what lies behind the idea of 'type' - in both mathematics and computer science. Many modern modellers first encounter this concept in the context of libraries like Numpy, which requires an array to be initialised with a 'dtype': an integer, say, or a floating point number of some kind. More sophisticated users soon discover that there are branching and even 'dependent' types that allow the same buffer to be written over with many different kinds of objects without restructuring the underlying array (which is presumptively expensive). Requiring such needs to be flagged in advance is what allows the standard numerical computing libraries to be so extraordinarily fast (at least compared to Python).

Let's us stipulate that each imperative must publish a sort of 'declaration of intent' when it is created. The declaration is issued voluntarily, but thereafter binds the imperative to conduct itself in a certain way during the write stage. By default, this declaration could simply be "I will submit my bill of works as a sealed envelope which I expect to be delivered directly to the Imperator without modification, and I reserve the write to include in that bill any modifications to any part of the data that I see fit." That is effectively what each imperative has been implicitly 'declaring' up to this point.

Opportunities for collaboration, however, may open up when imperatives are more forthcoming about their intentions. A particular imperative might instead declare "I may need to alter statelet `x`, statelet `y`, or statelet `z`, but never all three at once, and I when I submit my bill of works, I will always write which one I'm altering on the front of the envelope for everyone to see." If multiple different imperatives in the same routine make declarations of this sort, then there is an opportunity to do some 'mail-sorting' (rather 'bill-sorting') before the Imperator receives a single request. This could be done at the level of the routine, by a magistrate with appropriate authority, and could involve creating a totally new bill of works, where each submitted bill is emptied into a single envelope that encompasses the full set of changes required, without any clashes or duplication. This would have to be done at runtime, of course (since the outcome pivots based on the configuration of the state and the consequent actions of the imperatives), but the procedure for doing so could be 'baked in' at runtime for a potentially substantial speed boost.

As with the predicate logic, the richer the 'declaration language', the more sophisticated the potential synergies. Work is progressively shifted from runtime to compile time as more and more about the conduct of the program can be inferred ahead of time. Given that there is presumably some sort of 'write protocol' in play as well (the language that the bills themselves are written in for consideration by the Imperator), there is potentially a great deal of detail that could be included in these declarations: information about type, ordering, and the different circumstances in which this or that operation will be required to take place. The declaration language could even break the statelets themselves into smaller parts (creating 'statons', an even finer degree of discretisation), or publish flags signalling certain behaviours or observations that other declarations could conditionally respond to.

The danger with an advanced declaration system like this is not so much a lack of control as accidentally acquiring too much control, and inadvertently duplicating the same features the the program at large already supports. At that point, it becomes evident that the imperatives themselves need to be broken up into more granular imperatives - which could then be recombined as routines. Thus the process of creating and carefully considering the implications of new routines can not only create implementation efficiences, but - as we found with our predicate graph - also suggest better ways to analyse the program itself. The ultimate goal is to drive progressively towards the most granular possible form, with all the opportunities for efficient resynthesis that that brings.
